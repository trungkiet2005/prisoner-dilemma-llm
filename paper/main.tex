\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Language models are routinely said to play tit-for-tat, always defect or
win-stay lose-shift, and such labels now feed models of hybrid populations. Such
a label claims something about the process behind a trajectory, yet the
procedures that assign one answer for every input, whatever it contains. We put
4{,}800 agent-games from four models in the iterated prisoner's dilemma through
a read-out that asks deductively whether one canonical rule reproduces the whole
trajectory, reports the set when several do, falls back on a classifier when
none does, and abstains when that classifier is unsure. On synthetic play the
deductive stage is correct 99.8\% of the time and the abstention removes 59\% of
the residual error. On the corpus a rule is proved in 25.5\% of agent-games,
92\% of them AllC or AllD, whereas a \emph{reactive} rule is proved in 2.0\% and
assigned in 30.3\%, a gap robust to horizon controls. Nor is the mix a
property of the agent: rescaling every payoff, which cannot change the game,
moves it by up to 0.34 in total variation. Mining the unnamed quarter recovers
two-phase play opening with a probe, and behind it drift. We propose a reporting
standard for strategy labels.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} machine behaviour; large language models; iterated
prisoner's dilemma; strategy identification; selective classification;
evolutionary game theory; multi-agent systems

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as \emph{agents} that
negotiate, coordinate and act on behalf of users
\citep{park2023generative,dafoe2021cooperative}. Their behaviour there emerges
from strategic interaction rather than from isolated outputs, and is shaped by
training, prompting, role assignment and linguistic framing, with direct
implications for safety and governance. The proposal that machines be studied as
behaving entities, with the observational tools of ethology and the experimental
discipline of the behavioural sciences, has become considerably more urgent as
the population of interacting artificial agents has grown
\citep{rahwan2019machine}.

The iterated prisoner's dilemma is the natural testbed for that programme, and
its appeal is specific: its strategy space is mapped. A two-by-two matrix with
$T > R > P > S$ and $2R > T + S$ generates a landscape in which reciprocity,
forgiveness, provocability and exploitation are defined precisely and their
evolutionary fates computed
\citep{axelrod1981evolution,axelrod1984evolution,nowak1992tit,nowak1993strategy,nowak2006five,press2012iterated},
and it underpins work on the statistical physics of cooperation
\citep{szabo2007evolutionary,perc2010coevolutionary,perc2017statistical} and on
mechanisms such as intention recognition and commitment that displace the
cooperative equilibrium \citep{han2011intention,han2013good}. Because the map
exists, importing it is irresistible, and a rapidly expanding literature now
evaluates LLMs through game-theoretic lenses, reporting departures from Nash
equilibria, cooperative biases, model-specific signatures and archetypes
recovered from long-horizon play
\citep{akata2023playing,phelps2023investigating,fontana2024nicer,mei2024turing,lore2024strategic},
alongside probes with the instruments of cognitive psychology
\citep{binz2023using} and models used as simulated economic subjects
\citep{horton2023large,aher2023using,argyle2023out}.

The output of that literature is, very often, a name. Model $X$ plays
tit-for-tat; model $Y$ is a defector; model $Z$ resembles win-stay lose-shift. A
name of this kind is not a summary statistic. It is a claim about the process
that produced a trajectory, and it licenses inferences that no rate licenses:
that the agent will retaliate, that it will forgive, that it can be placed in a
replicator equation and its fate computed. The claim is therefore worth exactly
as much as the procedure that established it, and the procedures in use share a
property that is rarely stated. A classifier returns its argmax for every input;
a nearest-archetype distance returns a nearest archetype for every input.
Neither can answer ``none of these'', and neither distinguishes a trajectory a
rule reproduces exactly from one that merely lies closer to that rule than to
the others.

This paper takes strategy attribution itself as the object of study. We build a
read-out with four possible verdicts rather than one, apply it to a fully
crossed corpus of 2{,}400 dyads, and ask three questions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item How much of LLM play in the iterated prisoner's dilemma can be shown,
      rather than guessed, to follow a canonical strategy, and which strategies
      survive that test?
\item Is the resulting distribution over strategies a property of the model, or
      does it move with features of the prompt that leave the game unchanged?
\item What is the play that the read-out declines to name, and is there a
      strategy in it that the canonical vocabulary is missing?
\end{enumerate}

What makes the questions answerable is that the first stage of the read-out is
deductive. All four canonical rules are memory-one, so asking whether a
trajectory is exactly what AllC, AllD, tit-for-tat (TFT) or win-stay lose-shift
(WSLS) would have played is a lookup, and only a unique match supports the
statement that the agent played that rule. Several matching is an answer rather
than a failure: an agent that cooperates for ten rounds against a cooperator
\emph{is} AllC and TFT and WSLS at once. Only where that stage returns nothing
does a long short-term memory (LSTM) classifier \citep{hochreiter1997long}
supply a nearest neighbour, and only when its posterior clears a floor. The four
verdicts are reported separately throughout, never pooled.

Our contributions are: \textbf{(i)} a strategy read-out that separates deduction
from inference and can abstain, validated on synthetic play where the generating
rule is known and on a held-out rule that shows what an unseen strategy does to
a classifier that cannot abstain (\S\ref{sec:validation}); \textbf{(ii)} a
census showing that deduction reaches a quarter of the corpus, that 92\% of that
is constant play, and that reactive rules are labelled fifteen times more often
than they are demonstrated (\S\ref{sec:census}--\S\ref{sec:labels});
\textbf{(iii)} the finding that the strategy mix moves beyond a permutation null
under every manipulation we apply, including a positive affine rescaling of the
payoffs whose irrelevance is a theorem (\S\ref{sec:conditions}); and
\textbf{(iv)} a mining of the abstention set that recovers a two-phase,
switch-once structure absent from the canonical vocabulary, together with the
evidence that the rest of it is drift
(\S\ref{sec:abstention}--\S\ref{sec:verdict}).

\section{Methods}

\subsection{The game, the corpus and its manipulations}
\label{sec:stage}

FAIRGAME (Framework for AI Agents Bias Recognition using Game Theory)
\citep{fairgame2025} provides the computational infrastructure: conditions are
defined in JSON, and at runtime FAIRGAME combines them with language-specific
prompt templates, simulates repeated normal-form games and logs round-by-round
trajectories. We adopt it and add payoff scaling. Taking its multilingual
templates as given, rather than authoring our own, keeps the wording of the
manipulations out of our hands.

Agents played a symmetric two-player prisoner's dilemma
(figure~\ref{fig:setup}a). The templates state the cell values as
\emph{penalties}, years of imprisonment in the prisoner's-dilemma narrative, and
instruct the agent to \emph{minimise} them, so a smaller number is a better
outcome. Under that framing the base cells are $T = 0$, $R = 2$, $P = 6$,
$S = 10$, satisfying $T < R < P < S$, which is the penalty-space image of the
usual $T > R > P > S$. Option A is therefore the dominant, defecting action and
Option B is cooperation. On the utility scale $u = -\text{penalty}$ the two
normalised descriptors of the dilemma are greed $(T-R)/(T-S) = 0.20$ and fear
$(P-S)/(T-S) = 0.40$.

We flag the polarity explicitly because inverting it would exchange the AllC and
AllD columns of every table below. It is not a matter of interpretation:
FAIRGAME logs the realised payoff alongside every action, and the assignment
above reproduces the logged payoff on all 24{,}000 rounds of the corpus while
the inverted assignment reproduces none of them.

Three factors were crossed on top of that fixed game
(figure~\ref{fig:setup}b). Every payoff cell was multiplied by a scale
$\lam \in \{0.1, 1, 10\}$; because $\lam > 0$ this is a positive affine
rescaling of a von Neumann--Morgenstern utility
\citep{vonneumann1944theory,luce1957games}, so the outcome ordering, the
best-reply correspondence, the equilibrium set and the replicator dynamics are
unchanged and the three games are one game presented with different numbers. The
same game was instantiated in English, French, Vietnamese, Chinese and Arabic; a
game is not changed by the language its rules are stated in. Each agent received
a system-prompt persona, either \emph{cooperative} or \emph{selfish}, and the
four ordered pairings were crossed with everything else; a persona is not
irrelevant by theorem, but it carries no information about the game, since the
four pairings present identical payoff matrices, and an agent was never told its
opponent's persona. The realised scale was recovered per game from the values
logged rather than assumed from the directory.

The design crossed four models, Claude 3.5 Haiku, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, GPT-4o
and Mistral Large, with three payoff scales, five prompt languages, four persona
pairings and ten replicates: $4 \times 3 \times 5 \times 4 \times 10 = 2{,}400$
dyads, 600 per model. Each dyad ran for ten rounds and both agents are recorded,
giving 4{,}800 agent-games and 48{,}000 binary decisions, with exactly 40 dyads
in every model $\times$ scale $\times$ language cell. Every decision resolves to
one of the two option tokens and every dyad ran its full ten rounds, so no cell
is thinned by a failure to parse. One GPT-4o dyad is logged with a mixed persona
pairing where the design specifies a matched one, so that model's persona
margins are 599 and 601; no conclusion below turns on it.

On each round an agent received the full payoff table, its persona, the round
index and the complete history of both players' choices, and returned a single
token naming one of two options. Communication was disabled, so the only channel
between agents is the action history. Actions were labelled \texttt{OptionA} and
\texttt{OptionB} in all languages, avoiding the connotations that ``cooperate''
and ``defect'' would carry; which label denotes cooperation was never stated and
has to be read off the payoff table.

Decoding used a fixed low output-token budget with reasoning traces disabled.
This is not cosmetic: a model that emits hidden reasoning can exhaust a small
budget inside the trace and return a truncated string, and a harness that falls
back to a default action then produces an apparent cooperation rate of 1.0 that
is an artefact of the parser. Parse-failure and fallback rates were logged for
every cell and required to be below 0.02. Sampling temperature follows each
provider's recommended default, as in the FAIRGAME protocol: 1.0 for Claude,
Gemini and GPT-4o, 0.3 for Mistral Large.

\label{sec:confounds}
Two features of the design are confounded with model identity, and we declare
them here rather than leave them to be discovered. The Gemini arm was run with
the round count disclosed and the other three with the horizon hidden, so the
Gemini column is a ``model $\times$ horizon'' column and is marked as such in
figure~\ref{fig:setup}b. Sampling temperature, fixed by the provider default
above, likewise cannot be separated from model identity. Because both are
constant within a model, neither can generate any within-model effect reported
below; between-model comparisons carry them. The consequence we invoke wherever
it bites is that no read-out statistic is comparable \emph{across} models when
decoding stochasticity would itself move the comparison, which includes the
coverage figures of \S\ref{sec:census} and \S\ref{sec:abstention}. The claims we
draw there are about the panel, not about its ordering.

\subsection{A read-out with four verdicts}
\label{sec:readout}

A player's trajectory is encoded as a sequence of two-letter tokens,
$\langle$previous-round outcome$\rangle\langle$action this round$\rangle$, with
outcome letters $E$ (no history), $R$ (both cooperated), $S$ (the player
cooperated and was exploited), $T$ (the player exploited) and $P$ (mutual
defection). This is exactly the encoding of the synthetic corpus, which is what
makes a classifier trained on generated play applicable to a transcript.

\paragraph{Stage one: deduction.} Each canonical rule prescribes an action for
every outcome letter: AllC cooperates everywhere, AllD defects everywhere, TFT
plays $C$ after $E,R,T$ and $D$ after $S,P$, and WSLS plays $C$ after $E,R,P$
and $D$ after $S,T$. Asking whether a trajectory is exactly what rule $k$ would
have played is therefore a per-token lookup, checked without any learning. The
matcher returns a \emph{set}: exactly one member supports the statement that the
agent played that rule, and several members is the correct answer when the
realised history cannot separate them.

\paragraph{Stage two: inference, with the option of silence.} Where no rule
fits, an LSTM classifier trained on synthetic play from the four known
generating rules returns a posterior over them, and we take its argmax as the
\emph{nearest} rule, never as an identification. The network is a single-layer
LSTM (24-dimensional embedding, 96 hidden units) trained on the union of the
noise-free and 5\%-execution-noise splits of the ten-round synthetic corpus;
nothing is retrained on LLM play. Its label is accepted only when the top
posterior reaches a floor of 0.90, and otherwise the read-out abstains.

The floor is a convention here rather than a discovery. On thirty-round play the
posterior-confidence distribution is strongly bimodal and 0.90 falls in the gap;
at the ten-round horizon of this corpus it does not, so the value cannot be
defended as a valley. We defend it instead by the risk-coverage curve of
\S\ref{sec:validation}, which we report in full, and give the census at floors
of 0.80, 0.90 and 0.95 so that no conclusion rests on the cut.

Four verdicts therefore leave the read-out for every agent-game:
\emph{deduced} (exactly one rule reproduces the trajectory), \emph{rule set}
(several do), \emph{nearest rule} (none does and the classifier is confident)
and \emph{abstained} (none does and the classifier is not). They are never
pooled (figure~\ref{fig:setup}c). We also record the minimum number of rounds on
which play departs from its own nearest rule, which measures how much a
nearest-neighbour label is being asked to absorb.

Grim trigger is a fifth memory-one rule and is treated asymmetrically, which we
flag because the asymmetry runs against us: it enters every distance but not the
exact-match vocabulary, so a trajectory only grim trigger would reproduce counts
as unmatched. Admitting it raises the share of games matched by at least one
rule by 0.0, 0.2, 3.3 and 0.6 percentage points for Claude, Gemini, GPT-4o and
Mistral, and lowers Gemini's uniquely matched share by 0.5 points. It is
memory-one, $(1,0,0,0)$ opening with $C$, so it is inside the 32-rule class of
\S\ref{sec:abstention} throughout.

\subsection{Validating the read-out where the truth is known}
\label{sec:validation-method}

The synthetic corpus is generated by replaying each of the four rules against
sampled opponents, with execution noise at 0\%, 5\%, 10\% and 20\%; the network
sees only the first two. Because the generating rule is recorded, three
quantities that cannot be measured on LLM play are available there: whether the
deductive stage recovers the truth when it fires, how accuracy grows with rounds
observed, and what the abstention buys. We report the risk-coverage curve, the
accuracy of the deductive stage conditional on firing, and the identifiability
curve against its Bayes ceiling, the accuracy of an oracle that knows the
generating distribution and is limited only by trajectories several rules
produce alike.

A fourth quantity requires a rule the network has never seen. Generous
tit-for-tat, which forgives a defection with fixed probability, was held out of
training entirely and serves as a positive control for novelty: whatever the
classifier does with it is what it will do with any strategy outside its
vocabulary.

\subsection{Widening the vocabulary, and the nulls}
\label{sec:widening}

A rule may fail to fit because the vocabulary is too narrow rather than because
the play has no rule in it, so the abstention set is mined with three
progressively wider hypothesis classes, each paired with a null. The first is
all 32 deterministic memory-one rules, an opening move crossed with an action
for each of the four conditioning states. The second admits trajectories built
from two such rules with a single switch. The third is a library of fifteen
named non-canonical strategies from the tournament literature, including
suspicious TFT, grim trigger, tit-for-two-tats, gradual, prober, contrite TFT,
soft and hard majority and the alternator, together with the two-phase clock
rules spliced from an ordered pair of opponent-only strategies. Only AllC, AllD
and TFT can be spliced this way, since WSLS reads the player's own last action,
which gives six ordered pairs at nine switch points and 69 candidate rules in
all. That count is why the null matters: with 69 chances to fit a ten-round
binary sequence, a coverage figure means nothing until the same search has been
run on shuffled play.

Every coverage figure is paired with a permutation null in which the focal
player's own action sequence is shuffled in place, holding its cooperation rate
and the opponent's realised sequence fixed. The null is not a formality. A
two-segment fit over ten rounds has enough freedom to reproduce a large share of
shuffled play by chance, and constant play is invariant under the shuffle by
construction, so raw coverage on its own is not evidence of structure.

Two further probes ask what the play is without assuming a vocabulary at all.
The first counts every four-round window of the focal player's own actions and
compares its frequency with the same within-game shuffle, so a lift above one is
temporal structure and nothing else; alternation is the pattern a memory-one rule
cannot express, so the CDCD and DCDC windows are the ones to read first. The
second scores nested predictors of the next action, memoryless, memory-one,
memory-two and a memory-one rule allowed to change with the phase of the game,
by the Bayesian information criterion \citep{schwarz1978estimating} on identical
rows, so the comparison is about history rather than sample size.

Finally, we ask whether the unnameable play is a strategy at all. A trajectory
generated by a fixed rule is coherent across a game by construction, so we split
each game at round five and correlate the cooperation rate of the two halves
within each verdict class, and we cluster the abstention set in memory-one
profile space, sweeping $k$ from 2 to 8 and reporting silhouette alongside a
bootstrap adjusted Rand index.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{sec:stats}

All intervals are 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals over 2{,}000 resamples
that resample whole dyads \citep{efron1993introduction}. This is consequential:
rounds within a game are autocorrelated and the two agent-rows of a dyad share a
history, so a row-level resample would understate the interval by roughly
$\sqrt{2}$.

Whether a manipulation moves the strategy mix is answered with a distributional
test rather than a contrast on a single share. For each model and factor we take
the largest total-variation distance between the five-category mixes of any two
levels, and compare it with a permutation null that shuffles the factor label
across \emph{dyads}, so a dyad keeps both of its agent-rows together; 2{,}000
permutations are drawn. We report the observed distance, the permutation $P$
value and the null's 95th percentile, which doubles as the smallest shift the
test could have detected.

We do not correct for multiple comparisons and say so rather than leave it to be
inferred. Each model is a separate system and is treated as its own family, so
the tests within a manipulation are four families of one rather than one family
of four; the claims we draw are in any case about whether manipulations move the
mix at all, not about which model moves most.

\subsection{What the read-out is worth}
\label{sec:validation}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig2_readout.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Validation on synthetic play, where the generating rule is
known.}
(\textbf{a}) Accuracy as a function of the number of rounds observed, against
the Bayes ceiling of an oracle that knows the generating distribution and is
limited only by trajectories several rules produce alike. Ten rounds are enough
for the classifier to reach 0.984 against a ceiling of 0.993, so what fails on
LLM play in figures~\ref{fig:labels}--\ref{fig:hidden} is not the horizon.
(\textbf{b}) The risk-coverage curve. Sweeping the posterior floor trades
coverage for error among the games still labelled; squares mark labelling every
game and rings mark the 0.90 floor used throughout. On play 10\% of whose
actions are noise, a rate the network never saw in training, abstaining on 9\%
of games removes 59\% of the error.
(\textbf{c}) What a classifier does with a rule it has never seen. Generous
tit-for-tat was held out of training entirely; 90\% of its games are labelled
TFT and none is flagged as new. Any read-out without an abstention will absorb
an unknown strategy into its vocabulary rather than report one.}
\label{fig:readout}
\end{figure}

The deductive stage is exact by construction, but exactness is not usefulness:
it could fire so rarely as to be decorative, or so often as to be
uninformative. On the synthetic test set it fires on 80.3\% of trajectories with
a unique answer and recovers the generating rule in 99.8\% of those; a further
0.7\% receive a set, which contains the truth 99.4\% of the time; the remaining
19.0\% match nothing, which is what 5\% execution noise does to an exact matcher
over ten rounds. Deduction is therefore both frequent and near-certain when the
play really is rule-generated. This is the benchmark against which the LLM
figures of \S\ref{sec:census} should be read.

Crucially, the deductive stage is not biased towards the constant rules at this
horizon. Resolved by generating rule, it returns a unique answer on 79.7\% of
AllC-generated trajectories, 80.9\% of AllD, 80.8\% of TFT and 79.6\% of WSLS,
and where it fires it is right 99.8\%, 100.0\%, 99.6\% and 99.7\% of the time.
Ten rounds are enough to prove a reactive rule as often as a constant one, which
forecloses the reading that \S\ref{sec:labels} is a statement about the horizon
rather than about the play.

The classifier that covers the remainder needs ten rounds and no more
(figure~\ref{fig:readout}a). Accuracy rises from 0.487 after one round, where
the opening move alone separates AllD from the three rules that open
cooperatively, to 0.984 after ten, against a Bayes ceiling of 0.993. The gap to
the ceiling is 0.009. Whatever the read-out fails to do on LLM play is therefore
not a failure of horizon or of capacity on this vocabulary. Under execution
noise the classifier degrades gracefully within its training range, 0.995 at 0\%
and 0.973 at 5\%, and less gracefully outside it, 0.937 at 10\% and 0.810 at
20\%.

That degradation is what the abstention is for (figure~\ref{fig:readout}b). At
the 0.90 floor, the read-out declines to label 2.7\% of the low-noise test set
and error among the games it still labels falls from 1.65\% to 0.43\%. On the
unseen 10\%-noise split it declines 9.1\% of games and error falls from 6.34\%
to 2.61\%, a 59\% reduction. The floor is a chosen operating point on a curve
rather than a threshold with independent standing, which is why we report the
curve and repeat the census below at 0.80 and 0.95.

The held-out rule makes the sharpest case for having an abstention at all
(figure~\ref{fig:readout}c). Generous tit-for-tat was never shown to the
network. It is absorbed: 90.1\% of its games are labelled TFT, 5.1\% WSLS, 4.5\%
AllC and 0.3\% AllD. Nothing in the output says ``this is not one of my four''.
A read-out that must return a label will therefore report a vocabulary it was
given rather than a strategy the agent played, and it will do so with no
external sign that anything is wrong. This is not a defect of the particular
network; it is what forcing an argmax means.

\subsection{A census of named play}
\label{sec:census}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig3_labels.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{What a strategy label is made of.}
(\textbf{a}) The four verdicts per model and pooled. Dark: exactly one canonical
rule reproduces the whole trajectory. Mid: several do, and the ten rounds do not
separate them. Pale: none does, and the classifier supplies a nearest rule.
Lightest: none does and the classifier abstains.
(\textbf{b}) The same corpus resolved by label rather than by game. Each bar is
the share of agent-games carrying that name, split into the part a rule
reproduces exactly and the part that is a nearest-neighbour statement. AllC and
AllD are largely deduced; TFT and WSLS are almost entirely inferred.
(\textbf{c}) The same quantity per model. Bars mark the pooled value. Mistral
Large is assigned WSLS in 14.3\% of its agent-games and plays an exact WSLS
game in none of them.}
\label{fig:labels}
\end{figure}

Applied to the corpus, the read-out deduces a unique rule in 25.5\% of
agent-games, returns a set in 8.3\%, supplies a nearest rule in 40.5\% and
abstains in 25.7\% (figure~\ref{fig:labels}a). The spread across models is
large: Mistral Large is deduced in 48.7\% of its games and Claude 3.5 Haiku in
9.7\%, with Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and GPT-4o between at 21.9\% and 21.8\%.
Because sampling temperature is confounded with model identity
(\S\ref{sec:confounds}) we draw no conclusion from that ordering. The
panel-level statement is what matters and it does not depend on the ordering: in
every model at least half of the play is reproduced by no canonical rule at all,
from 50\% for Mistral Large to 86\% for Claude 3.5 Haiku.

The census is stable against the abstention floor in the direction that matters.
Moving the floor from 0.80 to 0.90 to 0.95 moves the abstained share from 20.0\%
to 25.7\% to 32.4\% and moves nothing else, because the deductive stage runs
first and is unaffected: the deduced and rule-set shares are 25.5\% and 8.3\% at
every floor. No claim below depends on where inside that band the cut is placed.

The composition of the deduced quarter is the first substantive result. Of the
1{,}224 agent-games in which exactly one canonical rule reproduces the whole
trajectory, 1{,}129, or 92.2\%, are AllC or AllD. The share of the corpus in
which a \emph{reactive} rule, TFT or WSLS, is deductively established is 95
agent-games, 2.0\%. Per model the constant share of the deduced set is 68.1\%
for Claude, 90.1\% for Gemini, 90.4\% for GPT-4o and 98.8\% for Mistral. What
the deductive stage mostly proves, in other words, is that an agent did the same
thing ten times. The rules that carry the theoretical content of the iterated
prisoner's dilemma, the ones that condition on what the opponent did, are almost
never proved.

One reading of that asymmetry has to be excluded before it can be believed. A
reactive rule is only distinguishable from a constant one when the opponent
gives it something to react to: against an opponent that never defects, TFT and
AllC prescribe the same ten actions and no method can separate them. We
therefore restrict to the agent-games whose opponent history affords the
distinction, playing at least one $C$ and at least one $D$ in rounds one to
nine. That is 66.1\% of the corpus, ranging from 50.1\% for Mistral Large to
88.4\% for Claude. Inside it the deduced-reactive share rises only from 2.0\% to
2.5\%, while the deduced-constant share is 18.0\%. The scarcity of proved
reactive play is therefore not an artefact of opponents who never gave these
agents an occasion to reciprocate: it survives conditioning on exactly those
occasions, at a horizon where the same matcher proves TFT on four fifths of
TFT-generated play (\S\ref{sec:validation}).

That composition also explains a result that would otherwise look damning for
the deductive stage. Against the shuffled null of \S\ref{sec:widening},
canonical coverage is barely enriched: the excess is 1.8 percentage points for
Claude, 1.5 for Gemini, 2.0 for GPT-4o and 0.4 for Mistral. It could not be
otherwise, because a constant trajectory is invariant under a permutation of its
own actions, so every AllC and AllD match survives the shuffle by construction
and those are 92\% of the matches. The enrichment test is therefore
uninformative about constant play and informative about reactive play, where it
says reactive matches are rare in the data and in the null alike.

\subsection{The labels that do not survive the audit}
\label{sec:labels}

Resolving the same corpus by label rather than by game is where the two stages
come apart (figure~\ref{fig:labels}b). AllC is assigned to 35.7\% of
agent-games and 33.4\% of those assignments are deductions; AllD is assigned to
25.6\% with 45.2\% deduced. TFT is assigned to 12.8\% of agent-games with 8.8\%
deduced, and WSLS to 17.5\% with 4.9\% deduced. Put the other way, 91.2\% of TFT
labels and 95.1\% of WSLS labels in this corpus are nearest-neighbour statements
about play that no rule in the vocabulary reproduces.

The gap is not a rounding artefact of a small denominator. Reactive labels are
handed out to 30.3\% of agent-games and reactive rules are demonstrated in
2.0\%, a factor of 15.3. Per model the ratio is 12.6 for Claude, 14.1 for
Gemini, 15.6 for GPT-4o and 33.1 for Mistral Large, whose WSLS label appears in
14.3\% of its agent-games and is deduced in none of its 1{,}200
(figure~\ref{fig:labels}c). A statement that Mistral Large plays win-stay
lose-shift is, on this corpus, a statement that its play was closer to WSLS than
to the other three, in games none of which WSLS would have produced.

What that proximity is worth can be quantified. Where a label is assigned by
nearest neighbour, play departs from the rule it names on 1.97 rounds out of ten
for AllC, 2.01 for AllD, 2.49 for TFT and 2.09 for WSLS. A rule that is wrong on
a fifth to a quarter of the decisions it is supposed to generate is not a
description of the process; it is the closest of four available descriptions,
three of which are also wrong.

Table~\ref{tab:profile} collects the per-model read-out together with the two
quantities the rest of the paper turns on, the shift in the mix under payoff
rescaling and the coverage the wider vocabulary buys on the abstained games.

This is a claim about warrant, not about the network. The classifier is accurate
where accuracy is definable (\S\ref{sec:validation}), and its nearest-neighbour
statements are not errors, since there is no truth for it to be wrong about. The
error is in reading one as an identification, which a single pooled label
distribution invites and reporting the four verdicts separately prevents.

\subsection{The strategy mix is a property of the prompt}
\label{sec:conditions}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig4_conditions.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The distribution over strategies moves with manipulations that
leave the game unchanged.}
(\textbf{a}) The strategy mix at each payoff scale, per model. Multiplying every
cell by a positive constant is a positive affine rescaling of a von
Neumann--Morgenstern utility, so the three games are one game and the predicted
shift is exactly zero. GPT-4o's AllD share falls from 0.57 to 0.24 across it.
(\textbf{b}) The same by assigned persona. Mistral Large told it is cooperative
plays AllD in 57\% of its games and AllC in 24\%; told it is selfish, 17\% and
62\%. The instruction moves the mix opposite to its own semantic content.
(\textbf{c}) Whether the mix moves at all, for every model and factor. Markers
give the largest total-variation distance between the mixes at two levels;
ticks give the 95th percentile of a permutation null that shuffles the factor
label across whole dyads. All sixteen comparisons exceed their null.}
\label{fig:conditions}
\end{figure}

A strategy label is meant to name something about the agent. If the distribution
over labels is instead a function of how the game was written down, a census
measures the prompt.

It is (figure~\ref{fig:conditions}). Every one of the sixteen model $\times$
factor comparisons moves the strategy mix beyond its permutation null. The
sharpest case is the payoff scale, because there the null is not a convenient
default but a theorem: multiplying every cell by $\lam > 0$ leaves best replies,
equilibria and replicator dynamics unchanged, so a strategic agent's mix must
not move at all. It moves by 0.263 for Claude, 0.338 for Gemini, 0.325 for
GPT-4o and 0.125 for Mistral in total variation, against null 95th percentiles
of 0.113, 0.123, 0.105 and 0.098 respectively, with $P < 0.001$ for the first
three and $P = 0.006$ for the fourth. Resolved into shares, GPT-4o is labelled
AllD in 56.8\% of its games at $\lam = 0.1$ and in 27.2\% at $\lam = 1$ and
24.2\% at $\lam = 10$; Claude's AllD share falls from 35.0\% to 10.2\% and
Gemini's from 23.2\% to 2.0\% across the same rescaling. Writing the same
payoffs as $0.2$ rather than $2$ roughly halves the share of play that is
recorded as unconditional defection.

Because ``Ambiguous'' is a statement about identifiability rather than about
behaviour, a shift in the five-category mix could in principle be games moving
into and out of that bin rather than a change in what the agents do. It is not.
Repeating every test over the four named strategies alone leaves all sixteen
comparisons above their nulls, with the payoff-scale distances at 0.245, 0.304,
0.296 and 0.127 and no $P$ value above 0.0065.

Prompt language moves the mix in all four models as well, by 0.188 to 0.296
against nulls of 0.142 to 0.175. Here the null is a weaker claim than for the
payoff scale, and one caveat is a finding rather than a nuisance: the FAIRGAME
templates do not state the objective identically in every language, since
English, French and Vietnamese instruct the agent to minimise a penalty while
Chinese and Arabic instruct it to maximise a reward, so a five-language
comparison mixes a language manipulation with an objective-framing manipulation.
We report the five-language test as the omnibus it is and do not decompose it,
because the strategy mix is the dependent variable here and the framing subset
leaves too few dyads per cell to estimate a five-category distribution stably.

The weakest of the sixteen comparisons is Gemini's language test, at 0.188
against a null 95th percentile of 0.175 ($P = 0.028$), and it is the one entry
that a family-wise correction across the four models would absorb. It is also
the one entry that the four-category re-test strengthens rather than weakens, to
0.205 at $P = 0.003$.

The persona manipulation produces the largest shifts in the study, and it is the
most interesting because it runs backwards. Mistral Large told it is cooperative
is labelled AllD in 57.0\% of its games and AllC in 23.5\%; told it is selfish,
the same model is labelled AllD in 16.5\% and AllC in 62.0\%. Claude moves from
32.0\% AllD and 27.8\% AllC to 5.5\% and 47.3\%. In every model the selfish
persona produces less unconditional defection than the cooperative one, and in
three of the four it produces more unconditional cooperation; GPT-4o is the
exception only in that its AllC share is flat at 0.26 while its AllD share falls
from 0.42 to 0.30. Resolving the manipulation into the four ordered pairings
moves the mix by 0.373 to 0.763, against own-persona shifts of 0.172 to 0.495.
An agent is never told its opponent's persona, so the excess of the pairing over
the own-persona effect is a response to the interaction rather than to the
instruction.

Both directions are consistent with a single reading, which we offer as the most
parsimonious account rather than as a demonstration: that the models treat the
displayed numbers as rewards to be maximised rather than as penalties to be
minimised, pattern-matching the matrix to the canonical reward-framed dilemma in
which the largest entry is the prize. Under that reading Option A yields the
symmetric pair $(6,6)$ and Option B can yield the single largest number, 10, so
an agent reaching for a mutually good outcome takes A and one reaching for the
largest attainable number takes B, which is what the persona columns show; and
enlarging $\lam$ makes that maximum more conspicuous, which is the direction the
scale panel shows. Separating it from an option-ordering account requires
restating the identical matrix in explicit reward form, an experiment we did not
run.

Whichever account is right, the consequence for attribution is the same and does
not depend on it. A strategy census is not a measurement of the agent unless it
is invariant under transformations that leave the game alone, and on this corpus
it is not.

\subsection{The anatomy of what the read-out will not name}
\label{sec:abstention}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig5_abstention.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The abstention set is structured, and the structure is outside
the vocabulary.}
(\textbf{a}) Which two rules the posterior is split between. Each bar is halved
between the pair. The network is not spreading mass over four labels; it is
caught between two, and TFT is in 63\% of the ties.
(\textbf{b}) Distance to the nearest canonical corner of the reactive square
$p = P(C \mid \text{opponent } C)$, $q = P(C \mid \text{opponent } D)$, in which
AllC, TFT and AllD are corners. Bars are means and whiskers the interquartile
range. Deduced play sits on a corner by construction; abstained play sits in the
interior.
(\textbf{c}) Frequency of four-round windows of the focal player's own actions,
relative to a shuffle of that player's own actions within its own game. Runs and
a single change of regime are enriched; alternating play, the pattern a
memory-one rule cannot express, is not.}
\label{fig:abstention}
\end{figure}

A quarter of the corpus leaves the read-out with no label. The easy dismissal is
that this residual is noise; answering it decides whether the vocabulary is too
small or the wrong shape.

It is not a shrug (figure~\ref{fig:abstention}a). The posterior in the abstained
games is not spread over four labels but split between two: the top pair is AllC
versus TFT in 29.9\% of cases, TFT versus WSLS in 19.0\%, WSLS versus AllD in
18.9\%, AllC versus WSLS in 17.2\% and TFT versus AllD in 14.3\%, with AllC
versus AllD, the only pair with no rule between them, at 0.6\%. TFT appears in
63.2\% of the ties. The network is refusing to choose between adjacent rules,
not failing to see anything.

Nor is the play near the vocabulary (figure~\ref{fig:abstention}b). In the
reactive square, where AllC, TFT and AllD are corners, mean distance to the
nearest corner is 0.024 for deduced games, 0.333 for those given a nearest rule
and 0.417 for those abstained, with medians of 0.000, 0.320 and 0.471 on a
square whose corners are at most 1.41 apart. The ordering is the point: a
nearest-neighbour label is assigned to play a third of the way to the nearest
canonical point, and the games the read-out abstains on are further still.

The motif analysis says what kind of structure it is
(figure~\ref{fig:abstention}c), and it contradicts the most natural guess. A
plausible hypothesis for unnameable play in a two-action game is alternation,
since a strictly alternating sequence is invisible to a memory-one rule. It is
not what is there. Relative to a within-game shuffle that preserves each
player's own cooperation rate, the four-round windows enriched in the abstention
set are the runs and the single switch: DDDD at a lift of 1.59, CCCC at 1.46 and
DDCC at 1.25, while CDCD sits at 1.08 and DCDC at 0.92, which is chance. Only
9.4\% of abstained games alternate more than their own base rate implies by a
margin of 0.15 or more, and pooled over the set the alternation excess is
negative, $-0.036$ ($\ci{-0.046}{-0.027}$): these agents perseverate. Whatever
the abstention set is made of, it is made of long runs with a change of regime
in them.

\subsection{Two-phase play, opening with a probe}
\label{sec:hidden}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig6_hidden.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{What the abstained play is, and whether it is a strategy.}
(\textbf{a}) Share of games reproduced exactly by the extended vocabulary of
fifteen named non-canonical rules plus 54 two-phase clock rules, against the
permutation null that shuffles the focal player's own actions. The wider
vocabulary earns 0.33 of coverage above chance on the abstained games and 0.21

(\textbf{b}) The rules that reproduce an abstained game exactly. Two thirds of
the fits are two-phase, the median switch falls at round two, and the traffic
runs towards cooperation.
(\textbf{c}) Correlation between the cooperation rate of the first and second

\label{fig:hidden}
\end{figure}

Widening the vocabulary answers the question the canonical four cannot
(figure~\ref{fig:hidden}a). The 69 candidates of \S\ref{sec:widening} reproduce
43.3\% of the abstained games exactly,
against a shuffled null of 10.1\%, an excess of 33.2 percentage points. On the
games the classifier was confident about, the same vocabulary reproduces 34.4\%
against a null of 13.3\%. Per model the abstained coverage is 27.3\% for Claude,
55.7\% for Gemini, 39.3\% for GPT-4o and 59.1\% for Mistral, against nulls of
9.6\%, 14.0\%, 6.7\% and 9.7\% in the same order. For 90.2\% of
the abstained games the nearest rule in the wide library is not one of the
canonical four.

What lands is specific (figure~\ref{fig:hidden}b). Of the 534 abstained games
the extended vocabulary reproduces exactly, 66.9\% are two-phase rules with a
single switch and the median switch falls at round two. The single most frequent
fit is AllD followed by AllC, at 39.1\% of the exact fits; then suspicious TFT,
which opens with defection and reciprocates thereafter, at 18.7\%; then TFT
followed by AllD at 18.7\%, TFT followed by AllC at 4.7\%, grim trigger at
3.7\%, the alternator at 2.6\%, AllC followed by AllD at 2.4\% and prober at
1.9\%.

Two features of that list are worth separating from the arithmetic. The first is
its direction: 65.5\% of the fitted switches end in a more cooperative regime
than they began, against 31.7\% that end in a less cooperative one and 2.8\%
that end in TFT. Backward
induction on a finite horizon predicts the opposite, and the switches do not
fall where an endgame effect would put them, but at a median of round two.
Whatever produces them is an opening phenomenon.

The second is that three of the four most frequent fits share a shape: an
opening defection that is not sustained. AllD-then-AllC, suspicious TFT and
prober all begin by defecting and then hand control to something else, and
together they account for 59.7\% of the exact fits. Read with the persona
inversion of \S\ref{sec:conditions}, the natural description is a probe: the
opening move is used to sample the opponent rather than to commit to a line of
play, and the strategy proper starts on round two or three. That shape is not in
the canonical vocabulary at any width, because it is not memory-one; it is a
rule about the round index. It is also not what any of the standard archetypes
would predict, and it is the substantive answer to what LLM play in this corpus
consists of when it is not constant.

The mechanism behind the failure of the memory-one class is visible directly.
Scoring nested predictors of the next action on identical rows, all four models
select memory-two by BIC: the improvement over a memoryless baseline goes from
$-1685$ to $-2399$ for Claude, $-4334$ to $-4792$ for Gemini, $-3684$ to $-4865$
for GPT-4o and $-6652$ to $-9158$ for Mistral. Allowing the memory-one rule to
change with the phase of the game instead, an equally sized extension in the
direction of time rather than of history, is worse than memory-two for every
model. The rows are pooled within a model, and a heterogeneous population of
memory-one agents can look memory-two when pooled, so this is evidence that the
corpus is not memory-one rather than that any single agent-game is.

One limit applies to the wider vocabulary itself and bounds how much of this can
be read as identification. Over ten rounds a trajectory rarely visits all four
conditioning states, so rules that differ only in an unvisited state cannot be
told apart. Of the 4{,}800 agent-games, 2{,}608 match none of the 32
deterministic memory-one rules, and of those that do match, most are matched by
four or eight at once; exactly 89, or 1.9\% of the corpus, are pinned to a single
one. A coverage figure for the wider class is a statement about a \emph{set} of
rules and not about a rule, and the ten-round horizon is what makes it so.

\subsection{Strategy, or drift}
\label{sec:verdict}

Whether the unnameable play is a strategy the vocabulary is missing or no
strategy at all has an answer the data can bound, and it is both
(figure~\ref{fig:hidden}c).

A trajectory generated by a fixed rule is coherent across the game by
construction, and the deduced games confirm the instrument: the correlation
between the cooperation rates of the two halves of a game is 0.985 there. It is
0.337 among games given a nearest rule and 0.315 among abstained games. The
first half of an abstained game barely predicts its second, and, importantly,
neither does the first half of a game the classifier was confident about. This
is not a property of the abstention set alone; it is a property of all LLM play
in this corpus that no rule reproduces.

Clustering finds no fifth archetype. In memory-one profile space the silhouette
of a $k$-means partition of the abstention set falls from 0.378 at $k = 2$ to
0.319 at $k = 8$ and never rises above its value at $k = 2$, so there is no
number of clusters the data prefer. The bootstrap adjusted Rand index is high at
small $k$, 0.93 at $k=2$ and 0.94 at $k=3$, but that measures whether a
partition is reproducible rather than whether it is separated, and a single
elongated cloud cut in half reproduces perfectly. The three-cluster solution is
interpretable, with one mode that persists and imitates (self-persistence 0.80,
opponent-matching 0.82, 56\% of the set), one near 0.5 on every coordinate
(31\%) and one that is anti-imitative (opponent-matching 0.12, 13\%), but these
are regions of a continuum rather than modes.

The verdict is therefore split, and we state it as such. About 43\% of the
abstention set is a rule outside the canonical four, most often a two-phase rule
with an opening probe, and that share is more than four times its shuffled null.
The rest of it, and the majority of the play the classifier labels confidently,
is within-game drift that no fixed rule will name, because there is no fixed
rule there to find.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{A label needs a provenance}

The central result is a gap between two quantities that are usually reported as
one. Reactive strategy labels are assigned to 30.3\% of the agent-games in this
corpus and reactive rules are deductively established in 2.0\% of them. Both
numbers are correct; they answer different questions, and only the second
supports the inferences that a strategy name is normally used to license.

The distinction is not pedantic, because the two behave differently under
everything else in the paper. The deduced share is unaffected by where the
abstention floor is placed, while the inferred share is not. The deduced share
is dominated by constant play, so it is nearly invariant under a permutation of
a player's own actions, which is why a null of that kind tests the reactive part
of the vocabulary and nothing else. And the deduced share is the only one for
which the validation of \S\ref{sec:validation} transfers, because it is the only
one for which ``correct'' is defined without reference to a training
distribution.

The remedy is cheap: an exact matcher costs nothing next to a classifier, and
reporting its output as a separate column costs nothing at all. We propose that
any strategy attribution for an artificial agent carry three shares, deduced,
several-rules-fit and nearest-neighbour, together with the mean number of rounds
on which play departs from the rule it was assigned. On this corpus those are
25.5\%, 8.3\%, 66.2\% and 1.97 to 2.49 rounds out of ten.

\subsection{The vocabulary is the wrong shape, not the wrong size}

The natural repair for a vocabulary that does not fit is to enlarge it, and the
way that repair fails here is informative. Going from four rules to all 32
deterministic memory-one rules and then to two of them in sequence leaves a
substantial residual, and the wide library that does earn 33 points over its null
earns them mostly with rules that are not memory-one at all but two-phase: a
regime for the opening and a regime for the rest, with a median switch at round
two. The BIC comparison points the same way, selecting memory-two over
memory-one for every model, and the motif analysis rules out the obvious
alternative, since alternation is at chance while runs and single switches are
enriched.

Together these say the class is misspecified rather than under-parameterised. A
memory-one description asks what an agent does after each joint outcome; these
agents are doing something that depends on where in the game they are as well as
on what just happened, concentrated in the first two or three rounds. A
vocabulary for LLM agents should therefore be built from phases and probes
rather than by adding rules to a memory-one enumeration.

The direction of the switches sharpens the point. Two thirds run towards
cooperation and they cluster at the opening, not the endgame. The canonical
prediction for a finite horizon is unravelling towards mutual defection; what
this corpus contains is an opening defection that is abandoned. That is closer
to exploration than to backward induction, and a population model assigning
these agents a fixed strategy would get both the sign and the timing wrong.

\subsection{Consequences for evolutionary models of hybrid populations}

Efforts to model populations in which artificial and human agents interact
\citep{paiva2018engineering,santos2018social,dafoe2021cooperative} typically
assign agents strategies from a known family and compute the resulting dynamics.
Three of our results bear on that programme. First, the assignment is mostly not
available: a replicator equation needs each agent placed at a point, and here
that point is deductively determined for a quarter of agent-games and is a
constant rule in 92\% of those. Second, it is not stable under transformations
the dynamics cannot see, since rescaling all payoffs leaves the replicator
equation identical and moves the mix by up to 0.34, so two population models a
game theorist would call the same model predict differently about the same
agent. Third, the individual game is not coherent enough to carry a label at
all, with a between-half correlation of 0.32 outside the deduced set. What these
support is a distribution over strategies whose support changes with framing,
not a strategy.

\subsection{Relation to the strategy-attribution literature}

The closest neighbours are studies that recover archetypes from LLM play:
\citet{fontana2024nicer} over long horizons, \citet{akata2023playing} as stable
model-specific signatures, and \citet{lore2024strategic}, who separate game
structure from contextual framing and find framing carries comparable weight.
Our results are not in tension with those measurements, and we do not claim
their labels are wrong. What we add is the denominator: an archetype recovered
from LLM play is a nearest-neighbour statement unless a deductive stage says
otherwise, and on our corpus it says otherwise for 2.0\% of reactive labels. The
framing sensitivity that \citet{lore2024strategic} document for outcomes we
document one level up, for the strategy distribution itself, under a
manipulation whose null is a theorem rather than a default. That also connects
the machine-behaviour programme \citep{rahwan2019machine} to the framing
literature in human decision making
\citep{tversky1981framing,kahneman1979prospect}, with an instructive disanalogy:
human framing effects map to a compact set of psychological mechanisms, whereas
what moves here is the identity of the strategy a standard read-out reports, and
it moves under a change of units.

\subsection{Limitations}

Four limitations bound these conclusions, and the first two are the ones that
would change the numbers rather than the interpretation.

The horizon is ten rounds, and this bounds \S\ref{sec:hidden} in particular. It
under-identifies the memory-one vocabulary, so most games matched by the wider
class are matched by a set of rules rather than by one and only 1.9\% of the
corpus is pinned to a single one of the 32; it gives a two-segment fit enough
freedom that a shuffled null already reaches 0.10, which is why every coverage
figure is reported against that null; and it compresses any endgame effect, so
the absence of endgame switching is weaker evidence than the presence of opening
switching. It does not, however, bound \S\ref{sec:labels}, and the two controls
that establish this are the ones we would put to a sceptical reader first: at
ten rounds the same matcher pins a unique answer on 80.8\% of TFT-generated and
79.6\% of WSLS-generated synthetic play, as often as on the constant rules, and
the deduced-reactive share barely moves when the corpus is restricted to the two
thirds of agent-games whose opponent history affords the distinction.

The model set is four, a small sample of a fast-moving population, and nothing
here establishes that the pattern generalises to models with explicit reasoning
procedures. Testing the read-out on reasoning models is the single most valuable
extension, and we regard these results as establishing the instrument rather
than settling the question.

Two confounds with model identity remain. The Gemini arm knew the round count
and the others did not, so that column mixes model identity with horizon
knowledge; sampling temperature is likewise fixed by provider default, so no
between-model ordering of a coverage statistic is interpretable and none is
claimed. Neither is constant across the levels of any manipulation, so neither
can generate the within-model effects of \S\ref{sec:conditions}.

Finally, the abstention floor is a convention at this horizon rather than a
valley in the posterior, and the reward-reading account of the persona and scale
effects (\S\ref{sec:conditions}) is an inference from converging evidence rather
than a controlled demonstration. We report the risk-coverage curve and the
census at three floors for the first, and identify the relabelling experiment
that would settle the second.

\subsection{Conclusion}

Four language models played 2{,}400 iterated prisoner's dilemmas, and a read-out
that separates deduction from inference and is allowed to abstain establishes a
canonical strategy for a quarter of the resulting agent-games, 92\% of it
unconditional cooperation or unconditional defection. Tit-for-tat and win-stay
lose-shift, the rules that carry the theoretical interest of the game, are named
fifteen times more often than they are demonstrated, and where they are named
the play departs from them on more than two rounds in ten. The distribution over
strategies is in any case not a property of the agent: it moves beyond a
permutation null under every manipulation we apply, including a rescaling of the
payoffs that cannot change the game. Mining the quarter the read-out will not
name recovers a structure the canonical vocabulary has no room for, a two-phase
rule switching once at a median of round two and running towards cooperation,
and behind it a majority of play that is drift rather than any rule at all.

The practical recommendation follows from the first result alone and costs
nothing to adopt. Run the deductive stage before the classifier, let the read-out
say ``no canonical rule explains this'', and publish the three shares and the
departure count alongside any strategy label. Until that is routine, the field
risks building an evolutionary game theory of artificial agents on names that
were assigned rather than earned.
