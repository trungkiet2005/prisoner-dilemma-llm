\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Large language models increasingly act as autonomous agents that negotiate,
delegate and compete, yet we lack a principled account of what their
behaviour in a social dilemma is. We report a fully crossed study of four
frontier models playing 2{,}400 iterated prisoner's dilemma dyads (48{,}000
binary decisions), crossing payoff magnitude, prompt language, assigned
persona and replicate. Two manipulations that game theory guarantees are
irrelevant nonetheless move behaviour: multiplying every payoff by a positive
constant, which leaves the game strategically identical, shifts cooperation
by up to 0.24, and changing the prompt language with objective framing held
fixed shifts it by up to 0.15. A third, the assigned persona, moves
cooperation by up to 0.35 while carrying no strategic information. Against
this, the design explains only 18.7\% of the variance in dyad-level
cooperation: replicates of an identical prompt diverge more than the
manipulations move the mean. The play is also not a strategy in the standard
sense. Three of four models end games more cooperative than they start,
contradicting backward induction, and no memory-one rule describes any model.
Reporting a cooperation rate, or a canonical strategy label, without
reporting framing sensitivity is not a measurement of the model.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} machine behaviour; large language models; iterated
prisoner's dilemma; cooperation; framing effects; evolutionary game theory

\section{Introduction}

The proposal that machines should be studied as behaving entities, with the
observational tools of ethology and the experimental discipline of the
behavioural sciences, rather than only as artefacts to be specified and
verified, has become considerably more urgent since it was made
\citep{rahwan2019machine}. Large language models now act as agents. They hold
budgets, place orders, write and review code, negotiate on behalf of users,
and increasingly encounter one another rather than a human counterpart. The
population of interacting artificial agents is growing faster than our
understanding of what governs their interaction, and the social dilemma is
the canonical setting in which that question is posed.

The prisoner's dilemma has served for four decades as the reference
experiment for the emergence of cooperation among self-interested agents
\citep{axelrod1981evolution,axelrod1984evolution}. Its appeal is that a very
small amount of structure --- a two-by-two payoff matrix with
$T > R > P > S$ and $2R > T + S$ --- generates a rich and well-mapped
strategic landscape, in which reciprocity, forgiveness, retaliation and
exploitation can be defined precisely and their evolutionary fates computed
\citep{nowak1992tit,nowak1993strategy,nowak2006five,press2012iterated}. That
landscape underpins a large body of work on the statistical physics and
evolutionary dynamics of cooperation
\citep{szabo2007evolutionary,perc2010coevolutionary,perc2017statistical}, on
the design of artificial agents that promote prosocial outcomes in mixed
human--machine populations
\citep{paiva2018engineering,santos2018social,dafoe2021cooperative}, and on
mechanisms such as intention recognition and commitment that shift the
cooperative equilibrium \citep{han2011intention,han2013good}.

It is therefore natural to ask where language models sit in that landscape,
and a rapidly expanding literature does exactly this. Models have been probed
with the instruments of cognitive psychology \citep{binz2023using}, compared
against human participants across a battery of canonical games
\citep{mei2024turing}, used as simulated economic subjects
\citep{horton2023large,aher2023using,argyle2023out}, and placed directly into
repeated dilemmas
\citep{akata2023playing,phelps2023investigating,fontana2024nicer}. The
resulting claims are typically of two kinds. The first is a scalar: model $X$
cooperates at rate $p$. The second is a label: model $X$ plays tit-for-tat,
or is unforgiving, or is nicer than humans.

Both kinds of claim presuppose something that has not been established. A
cooperation rate is a property of an agent only if it is stable under
transformations of the problem that do not change the problem. A strategy
label is informative only if the behaviour it names is actually generated by
something in the family of strategies from which the label is drawn. Neither
presupposition is safe for a system whose input is a natural-language string
and whose behaviour is known to depend on the surface form of that string in
ways that carry no task content
\citep{lu2022fantastically,sclar2024quantifying,deshpande2023toxicity,salewski2023context}.
Recent work has begun to make this concern concrete for strategic settings,
showing that contextual framing can matter as much as game structure for
model behaviour \citep{lore2024strategic}.

This paper takes the presupposition itself as the object of study. We adopt
two invariance principles as the test. The first is the affine invariance of
von Neumann--Morgenstern utility: if every payoff in a game is multiplied by
the same positive constant, the resulting game is strategically identical,
with the same best replies, the same equilibria and the same evolutionary
dynamics \citep{vonneumann1944theory,luce1957games}. Any agent whose
behaviour is described by a strategy over that game must be invariant to the
transformation. The second is the invariance of a game to the language in
which its rules are stated. A prisoner's dilemma described in French is the
same prisoner's dilemma as one described in Chinese; a model that plays them
differently is not responding to the game.

These two principles give us manipulations with an unusual property for
behavioural work: the null hypothesis is not merely a convenient default but
a theorem. If cooperation moves when payoffs are rescaled, the movement
cannot be a strategic response, because there is no strategic difference to
respond to. This converts a descriptive study into a test with a sharp
prediction, and it is what allows the results below to be stated as failures
rather than as observations.

We report a fully crossed factorial study in which four frontier language
models play 2{,}400 iterated prisoner's dilemma dyads, comprising 48{,}000
binary decisions. Payoff magnitude is crossed over three orders of magnitude,
prompt language over five languages, and assigned persona over four pairings,
with ten replicates in every cell. We ask three questions in sequence. Do the
models satisfy the invariances? How much of their behaviour does the design
explain at all? And is what remains describable as a strategy?

The answers are, in order, no, very little, and no. All four models violate at
least one invariance, two of them substantially. The four design factors
together account for 18.7\% of the variance in dyad-level cooperation, so
that repeated presentations of a byte-identical prompt disagree with one
another more than the deliberate manipulations move the average. And the
resulting play is not generated by a memory-one strategy: three of four
models finish games more cooperative than they begin, contradicting the
backward-induction prediction for a finite horizon, and the nearest canonical
rule is a poor description of the trajectory it names.

We do not read these results as showing that language models are bad at the
prisoner's dilemma. Optimal play is not the benchmark, and the models are not
being asked to optimise. We read them as showing that the standard summary
statistics of this literature --- a cooperation rate, an archetype label ---
are not yet measurements of the model, because they are not stable under
manipulations with respect to which any genuine measurement would have to be
stable. The contribution is a diagnostic, and a demonstration that it changes
what one is entitled to conclude.

\section{Material and methods}

\subsection{Stage game and payoff geometry}

Agents played a symmetric two-player prisoner's dilemma with base payoffs
$T = 10$, $R = 6$, $P = 2$, $S = 0$ (figure~\ref{fig:design}a). These satisfy
the two defining constraints, $T > R > P > S$ and $2R > T + S$, so that
mutual defection is the unique one-shot Nash equilibrium while mutual
cooperation is Pareto superior to it. Two normalised descriptors of the
dilemma's severity locate this matrix within the family: greed,
$(T-R)/(T-S) = 0.40$, is the incentive to defect against a cooperator, and
fear, $(P-S)/(T-S) = 0.20$, is the incentive to defect against a defector.

Every cell of the matrix was multiplied by a payoff scale
$\lam \in \{0.1, 1, 10\}$, spanning three orders of magnitude. Because
$\lam > 0$, this is a positive affine rescaling: greed, fear, the ordering of
outcomes, the best-reply correspondence and the equilibrium set are all
unchanged, and the three games are the same game presented with different
numbers.

The realised scale was recovered per game from the payoff values actually
recorded rather than assumed from the directory in which the game was stored.
This matters: 80 rounds (four dyads, 0.17\% of the corpus) were found to
carry $\times 1$ payoffs inside a folder nominally labelled $\times 10$, and
are labelled by their realised scale in all sensitivity analyses.

\subsection{Design}

The design crossed four models --- Claude 3.5 Haiku, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite,
GPT-4o and Mistral Large --- with three payoff scales, five prompt languages
(English, French, Vietnamese, Chinese, Arabic), four persona pairings and ten
replicates, giving 600 dyads per model and 2{,}400 in total
(figure~\ref{fig:design}b). Each dyad ran for ten rounds and both agents are
recorded, so the corpus contains 4{,}800 agent-games and 48{,}000 binary
decisions. Every model $\times$ scale $\times$ language cell contains exactly
40 dyads; the design is balanced by construction, not by post-hoc weighting.

Each agent received a system-prompt persona, either \emph{cooperative} or
\emph{selfish}, and the four pairings crossed the focal agent's persona with
its opponent's. Critically, an agent was never told its opponent's persona.
The opponent's persona is therefore a manipulation the focal agent can only
learn about through play, which gives a clean separation between what the
prompt does and what the interaction does.

\subsection{Prompting protocol}

Prompts were generated with the FAIRGAME framework \citep{fairgame2025},
which supplies matched game templates in the five languages. On each round an
agent received the full payoff table, its persona, the round index and the
complete history of both players' previous choices, and returned a single
token naming one of two options. Communication between agents was disabled,
so the only channel between them is the action history. Actions were labelled
\texttt{OptionA} and \texttt{OptionB} in all languages, avoiding the
connotations that words such as ``cooperate'' and ``defect'' would carry.

Decoding used a fixed low token budget with reasoning traces disabled. This
detail is not cosmetic. Models that emit hidden reasoning before answering
can exhaust a small output budget inside the trace, returning a truncated
string that fails to parse; if the harness then falls back to a default
action, the result is an apparent cooperation rate of 1.0 that is an artefact
of the parser rather than a behaviour. We logged the parse-failure and
fallback rates for every cell and required both to be below 0.02 before a
cell was admitted.

\subsection{Behavioural measures}

The primary measure is the cooperation rate, the proportion of a game's ten
rounds on which an agent chose the cooperative action. Because both agents of
every dyad appear as rows, the exploited (CD) and exploiting (DC) shares are
equal by construction, and composition analyses merge them into a single
anti-coordination band.

The memory-one fingerprint of an agent is the vector of four conditional
probabilities $\big(p(C \mid R),\, p(C \mid S),\, p(C \mid T),\,
p(C \mid P)\big)$, the probability of cooperating after each of the four
joint outcomes of the previous round. This is the complete description of a
memory-one strategy and places every canonical rule at a known point: AllC at
$(1,1,1,1)$, AllD at $(0,0,0,0)$, tit-for-tat at $(1,0,1,0)$, win-stay
lose-shift at $(1,0,0,1)$ and grim trigger at $(1,0,0,0)$. Two summaries are
derived from it: niceness, the probability of cooperating on round one, and
reciprocity, $p(C \mid C_{-1}) - p(C \mid D_{-1})$.

Payoff efficiency is the mean per-round payoff divided by $R$, the
mutual-cooperation benchmark, which makes it comparable across $\lam$.

\subsection{Strategy read-out}

Assigning a strategy label to observed play was done in two stages, which we
keep distinct because they license very different claims.

The first stage is exact and deductive. For each agent-game we enumerated all
32 deterministic memory-one rules and retained those consistent with every
transition actually observed. A game may be matched by exactly one rule, by
several, or by none. Only the first case supports the statement that the
agent played that rule.

The second stage is inductive and applies only where the first fails. A
long short-term memory classifier \citep{hochreiter1997long} trained on
synthetic play from known generating strategies returns a posterior over
archetypes, and we take its argmax as the nearest neighbour. Its calibration
was measured on held-out synthetic play against the Bayes ceiling implied by
identical prefixes carrying different labels; that calibration describes the
instrument, not the models, and we are explicit throughout about which of the
two stages produced a given number.

We also recorded, for each agent-game, the minimum number of rounds on which
play departs from its own nearest rule. This is the quantity that tells one
whether a label is a description or a gesture.

\subsection{Statistical procedures}

All intervals are 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals over 2{,}000
resamples that resample whole dyads \citep{efron1993introduction}. This is
deliberate and consequential. Rounds within a game are strongly
autocorrelated and the two agent-rows of a dyad share a history, so a
round-level or row-level resample would understate the interval by roughly
$\sqrt{2}$.

The payoff-scale slope is the change in cooperation rate per decade of
$\lam$, fitted by least squares on $\log_{10}\lam$. Language disparity is the
difference between a model's highest- and lowest-cooperation language, tested
against a permutation null that shuffles the language label across dyads
within a model over 5{,}000 draws while holding the number of dyads per
language fixed. Persona effects are differences of means with the same
dyad-level bootstrap, reported alongside Cohen's $d$.

The variance decomposition is sequential (Type I), entering factors in the
order model, payoff scale, language, persona. Sequential decomposition
credits shared variance to whichever factor is entered first, so entering
model identity first makes its share an upper bound --- the conservative
direction for the argument we make from it.

Memory depth was compared by the Bayesian information criterion
\citep{schwarz1978estimating} across memoryless, memory-one, memory-two and
memory-one-plus-phase specifications.

\subsection{Reliability audit}

Three checks were run whose expected answer is ``nothing here''. Agents 1 and
2 of a matched pairing receive identical prompts, so their cooperation rates
should agree; the observed correlation is $r = 0.67$--$0.95$ with a mean
position bias of $-0.017$, small but non-zero and carried into any claim that
rests on a single role. The ten replicates of a cell share a prompt, and
their spread is 1.3--1.6 times what independent rounds would produce, which
is why every interval in this paper resamples dyads. Cell means reproduce
across a random split of the dyads with Spearman--Brown reliability of
0.90--0.97, so the quantities we compare are stable even though individual
games are not.

\subsection{Two confounds declared in advance}

\paragraph{Horizon.} The Gemini arm was run with the round count disclosed to
the agents; the Claude, GPT-4o and Mistral arms were run with the horizon
hidden. A known finite horizon is precisely the condition under which
backward induction predicts unravelling, so the Gemini column is
``model $\times$ horizon'' and not ``model'', and it is marked as such in
figure~\ref{fig:design}b. As shown below, this confound works against the
conclusion we draw from it rather than for it.

\paragraph{Objective framing.} The FAIRGAME templates do not state the
agent's objective identically in every language. The English, French and
Vietnamese templates describe the outcomes as penalties and instruct the
agent to minimise them; the Arabic and Chinese templates describe the
outcomes as penalties but instruct the agent to maximise its rewards.
Language is therefore confounded with objective framing in the five-language
comparison, and we address this directly in \S\ref{sec:language} by measuring
the language effect within the three languages that share a single framing.
We flag it here because it is a property of a widely used multilingual
game-theoretic benchmark and not only of this study.

\subsection{Aggregate cooperation conceals more than it reports}

Pooled across the entire design, the four models cooperate at very different
rates: 0.55 for GPT-4o, 0.48 for Mistral Large, 0.38 for Claude 3.5 Haiku and
0.26 for Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite. Read naively, this is the headline of a
conventional benchmark report, and it is the form in which such results are
usually communicated.

It is worth establishing immediately how little that number carries. The
distribution of within-game cooperation is not concentrated near its mean for
any model. For GPT-4o the mean of 0.55 falls in the emptiest region of its
own distribution: the modal game is one in which the agent cooperates on
every round, 14.9\% of its games are entirely cooperative and 10.5\% are
entirely defecting. Mistral Large is more extreme still, with 24.3\% of games
entirely cooperative and 25.0\% entirely defecting, so that the mean of 0.48
describes a bimodal mixture rather than a typical game. Gemini's low mean of
0.26 is likewise produced substantially by a mass of 36.9\% wholly defecting
games rather than by uniformly guarded play.

The natural next question is how much of this spread the design accounts for.
The answer is that it accounts for very little. In a sequential
decomposition, model identity explains 9.5\% of the variance in dyad-level
cooperation, persona pairing 5.5\%, payoff scale 3.6\% and language 0.2\%,
for a total of 18.7\%. The remaining 81.3\% is within-cell: ten replicates of
a byte-identical prompt disagree with one another more than all four
deliberate manipulations together move the mean. Because model identity was
entered first, its 9.5\% is an upper bound.

This is the context in which the invariance tests should be read. We are not
asking whether the models differ; we are asking whether the differences that
manipulations produce are differences a strategic agent could have.

this section develops.

Multiplying every payoff by a positive constant leaves best replies,
equilibria and evolutionary dynamics unchanged. The null for a strategic
agent is therefore not approximately zero but exactly zero.

Two of the four models violate it decisively (figure~\ref{fig:invariance}a).
Claude 3.5 Haiku loses $0.120$ cooperation per decade of $\lam$
(95\% CI $\ci{-0.137}{-0.102}$) and GPT-4o loses $0.117$
($\ci{-0.140}{-0.094}$). Across the three scales this is a total swing of
0.24 and 0.23 respectively: GPT-4o cooperates at 0.71 when the same dilemma
is presented with payoffs $(1.0, 0.6, 0.2, 0)$ and at 0.47 when it is
presented with $(100, 60, 20, 0)$. Gemini shows a smaller but still
non-zero slope of $-0.030$ ($\ci{-0.053}{-0.007}$), and only Mistral Large is
consistent with invariance, at $-0.015$ ($\ci{-0.043}{0.012}$).

The direction is consistent and interpretable: three of four models cooperate
less as the stated stakes grow, even though the stakes are identical in every
sense that a game-theoretic account can express. Note also that the effect is
not monotone in the way a simple magnitude heuristic would predict. For
Claude and GPT-4o almost the entire swing occurs between $\times 0.1$ and
$\times 1$, with $\times 1$ and $\times 10$ nearly indistinguishable, which
suggests sensitivity to the appearance of the numbers --- fractional versus
integral, single- versus multi-digit --- rather than to magnitude as such.

Cooperation is not the only thing that moves. Payoff efficiency, measured
relative to the mutual-cooperation benchmark, tracks the same pattern, and
every one of the twelve model $\times$ scale cells sits above the efficiency
a dyad would obtain if it reached the same cooperation rate without ever
mistiming, by a premium of 0.033 to 0.088. The models earn more than
coordinated play at their own cooperation rate would yield, and they do so
through anti-coordinated rounds, which pay $(T+S)/2 = 5$ against $P = 2$ for
mutual defection. Aggregate payoff efficiency is thus a poor proxy for
cooperation here, and the two should not be used interchangeably.

\subsection{Prompt language changes behaviour, with framing held fixed}
\label{sec:language}

Measured across all five languages, three of four models show a language
disparity beyond its permutation null: 0.156 for Gemini ($P < 0.001$), 0.155
for Mistral ($P < 0.001$) and 0.087 for Claude ($P = 0.001$), against 0.040
for GPT-4o ($P = 0.70$).

That comparison is confounded, and we treat the confound as a result rather
than as a footnote. As set out in the methods, the English, French and
Vietnamese templates instruct the agent to minimise a penalty, whereas the
Arabic and Chinese templates instruct it to maximise a reward. Under the
literal penalty-minimising reading, the dominance relation between the two
options reverses relative to the reward-maximising reading, so a raw
comparison across all five languages mixes a language manipulation with an
objective-framing manipulation.

Two things follow. First, the models do not appear to be performing the
dominance calculation under either reading. In every one of the five
languages, agents told they are cooperative open with the same option far
more often than agents told they are selfish, with a gap of 0.42 to 0.73;
the action that functions as cooperative is the same one throughout,
regardless of whether the stated objective is to minimise or to maximise.
Models are tracking the semantic frame of the prompt rather than the
arithmetic of the objective they were given.

Second, the language effect must be re-tested with framing held fixed. Doing
so within English, French and Vietnamese alone (figure~\ref{fig:invariance}b,
solid bars) leaves the effect intact for two of four models: Mistral Large
retains a disparity of 0.152 ($P < 0.001$) and Claude 0.087 ($P = 0.002$),
both above their permutation nulls. Gemini's apparent disparity, by contrast,
collapses from 0.156 to 0.052 and ceases to be significant ($P = 0.19$),
identifying most of its five-language gap as a framing effect rather than a
language effect. GPT-4o remains invariant on both readings ($P = 0.64$).
The framing contrast itself is small and inconsistent in sign across models
($-0.064$ to $+0.093$), so it does not act as a simple main effect either.

The surviving effects are substantial. Mistral Large cooperates at 0.39 in
English and 0.54 in Vietnamese --- three languages, one objective framing,
one payoff matrix, a gap of 0.15 in cooperation. We emphasise that this is
now a claim about two of four models rather than three, and that the more
cautious claim is the one the data support.

\subsection{Assigned persona changes behaviour, and leaks}

The persona manipulation differs from the previous two in that it is not
strategically irrelevant by theorem; a persona could in principle be read as
information about the kind of player one is facing. But an agent's own
persona carries no information about the game, and the four persona pairings
present identical payoff matrices.

The effect is nonetheless the largest of the three
(figure~\ref{fig:invariance}c). Mistral Large cooperates 0.354 more when told
it is cooperative than when told it is selfish ($\ci{0.303}{0.408}$,
$d = 0.94$), and Claude 0.158 more ($\ci{0.127}{0.189}$, $d = 0.61$). Gemini
shows a small effect of 0.045 ($\ci{0.001}{0.086}$, $d = 0.14$) and GPT-4o
none at all ($-0.018$, $\ci{-0.067}{0.028}$). The effect is concentrated in
the opening move: told it is cooperative, Mistral opens cooperatively in 81\%
of games; told it is selfish, in 0\% of games. Claude moves from 86\% to
19\%, Gemini from 46\% to 2\% and GPT-4o from 62\% to 16\%.

Two observations complicate the reading that persona simply installs a
disposition. The first is that the opening it installs is not profitable.
For Mistral, opening cooperatively costs $1.82$ payoff units per round
($\ci{-2.15}{-1.47}$) relative to opening with defection; for the other three
models the difference is indistinguishable from zero. A persona that pushes
an agent towards a costly opening and no compensating downstream advantage is
behaving as a stylistic instruction rather than as a strategic commitment.

The second is that the opponent's persona --- never disclosed, inferable only
from play --- has a detectable effect for Gemini, which cooperates 0.080 more
against a cooperative opponent ($\ci{0.039}{0.123}$), and marginally for
Claude ($0.033$, $\ci{0.000}{0.067}$). For Gemini this opponent effect is
larger than its own-persona effect. That is the signature of an agent
responding to the interaction rather than to the instruction, and it is the
one place in this study where a model behaves more like a strategic player
than like a prompt-follower.

\subsection{The play is not a strategy}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig3_strategy.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{What the play is not.}
(\textbf{a}) Cooperation by round with 95\% bootstrap bands. Backward
induction from a finite horizon predicts decay towards mutual defection; only
Claude decays. Gemini is the single arm that was told the round count, and it
warms up rather than unravelling.
(\textbf{b}) The memory-one fingerprint: probability of cooperating after
each of the four joint outcomes of the previous round. These four numbers are
the complete description of a memory-one strategy. Dashes mark tit-for-tat,
which is 1 after CC and DC and 0 after CD and DD. No model approaches it: all
four cooperate readily after being exploited (CD), where every canonical rule
except AllC prescribes 0, and three of the four cooperate less after
exploiting (DC) than after being exploited, which inverts reciprocity.
(\textbf{c}) How an archetype label is actually reached. Dark: exactly one of
the 32 deterministic memory-one rules reproduces the whole trajectory. Mid:
several do, and the honest answer is the set. Grey: none does, and the label
is a nearest-neighbour statement. The right-hand column gives the mean number
of rounds out of ten on which play departs from its own nearest rule.}
\label{fig:strategy}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{No unravelling.} With a finite and commonly known horizon,
backward induction predicts defection in every round; with an uncertain
horizon the prediction weakens but the endgame effect remains the canonical
observation in human play. Only Claude 3.5 Haiku shows it, declining from
0.53 in round one to 0.26 in round ten, a change of $-0.27$
(figure~\ref{fig:strategy}a). The other three models end \emph{more}
cooperative than they start: GPT-4o by $+0.20$, Gemini by $+0.08$ and Mistral
by $+0.08$.

The horizon confound turns out to strengthen rather than weaken this
observation. If unravelling were being suppressed simply because agents did
not know when the game ended, the arm that did know should unravel. Gemini is
that arm, and it warms up. Whatever prevents backward induction here is not
ignorance of the horizon.

\paragraph{No memory-one rule.} The memory-one fingerprint places every model
far from every canonical strategy (figure~\ref{fig:strategy}b). The nearest
archetype is tit-for-tat for Claude at Euclidean distance 0.76 in the
four-dimensional fingerprint space, and grim trigger for Gemini (0.71),
Mistral (0.94) and GPT-4o (0.98). For scale, the canonical rules are at most
2.0 apart from one another in this space, so a distance of 0.7--1.0 is not a
near miss.

The shape of the departure is more informative than its size. All four models
cooperate readily after having been exploited --- $p(C \mid S)$ is 0.41 for
Claude, 0.66 for Gemini, 0.86 for GPT-4o and 0.89 for Mistral --- where
tit-for-tat, grim and win-stay lose-shift all prescribe 0. And three of four
cooperate \emph{less} after having successfully exploited the opponent than
after being exploited by it: $p(C \mid T)$ is 0.51, 0.19, 0.36 and 0.04
respectively, where tit-for-tat prescribes 1. Mistral Large's reciprocity
index is actually negative, $-0.149$, meaning it is more likely to cooperate
after the opponent defected than after the opponent cooperated. This is not a
degraded reciprocator; it is a different object.

Bayesian information criteria confirm that memory-one is the wrong family
rather than merely a poor fit. For all four models a memory-two specification
improves on memory-one by a substantial margin, indicating dependence on
history deeper than the previous round.

\paragraph{A label is mostly not a description.} Combining these, we ask how
often an archetype label is earned deductively rather than assigned by
nearest neighbour (figure~\ref{fig:strategy}c). Exactly one of the 32
deterministic memory-one rules reproduces the entire trajectory in 12.5\% of
Claude's agent-games, 17.3\% of GPT-4o's, 38.9\% of Mistral's and 40.8\% of
Gemini's. In the remaining 86\%, 72\%, 48\% and 55\% respectively, no rule in
the class fits and the label is a nearest-neighbour statement produced by the
classifier. Where a label is assigned this way, play departs from the rule it
names on 1.0 to 2.0 rounds out of ten.

A further caveat limits even the deductive fraction. Ten rounds rarely visit
all four conditioning states, so rules that differ only in an unvisited state
cannot be distinguished. Of the 4{,}800 agent-games, 2{,}192 match at least
one of the 32 rules but only 89 match exactly one. Coverage figures for the
wider class are therefore statements about a \emph{set} of rules, not about
a rule.

Widening the vocabulary helps, but not enough and not for free. Moving from
the four canonical archetypes to all 32 memory-one rules and then to a
one-switch two-regime model raises explained coverage to 0.80, 0.85, 0.73 and
0.58 for Gemini, Mistral, GPT-4o and Claude. But a shuffled null over the
same classes already reaches much of that, and the excess over chance is
0.02 for the canonical four, 0.07--0.13 for the 32 memory-one rules and
0.20--0.24 for the two-regime model. Between 15\% and 42\% of agent-games
remain unexplained by any of them.

\subsection{What is stable}

It would be a misreading to conclude that nothing here is measurable. The
quantities we compare are reliable even though individual games are not.
Split-half reliability of the cell means is 0.90 for Claude, 0.91 for GPT-4o,
0.95 for Gemini and 0.97 for Mistral. The models are also clearly distinct
from one another in fingerprint space and in their response to every
manipulation: the differences reported above are not noise.

What is unstable is the individual game. Ten replicates of an identical
prompt have a standard deviation 1.3 to 1.6 times what independent rounds
would produce, and within a dyad the two agents' realised payoffs differ by a
median of 5.0 units for Mistral and 3.0 for GPT-4o. The correct summary is
that these systems have reproducible \emph{propensities} and irreproducible
\emph{episodes}, and that a benchmark reporting the former as though it
characterised the latter is over-claiming.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What an invariance failure means}

The central results are failures of two invariances that are not empirical
regularities but consequences of how games are defined. Multiplying every
payoff by a positive constant produces the same game; stating a game in a
different language produces the same game. An agent whose behaviour is
described by a strategy over a game must therefore be invariant to both. Two
of four models move by more than 0.11 in cooperation per decade of payoff
scale, and two of four move by 0.09--0.15 across languages that share an
objective framing.

The consequence is not that the models are irrational in the ordinary sense
of choosing dominated actions. It is stronger and more specific: the object
that a cooperation rate is supposed to measure is not well defined for these
systems, because the measurement changes when the thing measured does not.
Reporting that a model cooperates at 0.55 without reporting that the same
model cooperates at 0.71 or 0.47 depending on whether the payoffs are written
as decimals or as hundreds is not a partial description; it is a description
of an artefact of presentation.

This connects the machine-behaviour programme \citep{rahwan2019machine}
directly to the framing literature in human decision making
\citep{tversky1981framing,kahneman1979prospect}. Humans also violate
invariance, and the discovery that they do reshaped economics. The
disanalogy is instructive, though. Human framing effects are systematic,
have been mapped to a compact set of psychological mechanisms, and are
reproducible across populations. What we observe here is heterogeneous across
models --- Mistral is scale-invariant but the most persona-steerable; GPT-4o
is language-invariant and persona-inert but strongly scale-sensitive --- and
sits on top of a within-cell variance that dwarfs it. There is no single
``LLM framing effect'' to be characterised, only a per-model, per-manipulation
profile that must be measured.

\subsection{Consequences for strategy attribution}

A large part of the appeal of the prisoner's dilemma is that its strategy
space is well mapped, and it is tempting to import that map wholesale when
describing language models. Our results suggest the import is not licensed.

The memory-one fingerprints do not merely fail to coincide with canonical
rules; they have a shape no canonical rule has. Cooperating readily after
being exploited and reluctantly after exploiting is the reverse of the
reciprocity that makes tit-for-tat evolutionarily interesting
\citep{axelrod1981evolution,nowak1992tit}. Mistral Large's negative
reciprocity index is not a noisy zero but a systematic inversion. Whatever
generates this behaviour, it is not a rule that conditions on the previous
round in the way the memory-one family does, and the BIC comparison confirms
that history dependence runs deeper than one round.

The practical recommendation is a reporting standard. A statement of the form
``model $X$ plays tit-for-tat'' should be accompanied by the fraction of
agent-games in which a rule was deductively established rather than assigned
by nearest neighbour, and by the number of rounds on which play departs from
its assigned label. On our corpus those numbers are 12--41\% and 1.0--2.0
rounds out of ten. Without them, an archetype label reports the resolution of
the classifier rather than the behaviour of the model.

\subsection{Implications for evolutionary and hybrid populations}

The results bear directly on the growing effort to model populations in which
artificial and human agents interact
\citep{paiva2018engineering,santos2018social,dafoe2021cooperative}. Such
models are typically built by assigning agents strategies from a known family
and computing the resulting dynamics. Two of our findings complicate that
programme.

First, if an artificial agent's cooperation depends on the units in which
payoffs are written, then its behaviour is not a function of the game and
cannot be carried across a replicator equation that only knows the game.
Two population models identical up to a rescaling of the payoff matrix would
make different predictions about the same artificial agent, which is not a
property any of these frameworks are built to accommodate.

Second, if 81\% of the variance in cooperation is within-cell, then the
appropriate representation of such an agent in a population model is not a
strategy but a distribution over strategies, and possibly one whose support
changes with framing. The absorbing behaviour we observe --- 25\% of
Mistral's games entirely defecting and 24\% entirely cooperative --- suggests
that the relevant dynamics may be better captured by mixtures with
absorbing states than by a single reactive rule. Whether the well-understood
results on the evolution of reciprocity
\citep{nowak1993strategy,nowak2006five,press2012iterated} survive that
substitution is, we think, an open and interesting question, and one this
theme issue is well placed to pose.

\subsection{Limitations}

Four limitations bound these conclusions, and we state them plainly.

The model set is the most consequential. Four models is a small sample of a
fast-moving population, and three of the four belong to an earlier generation
than the fourth. Nothing here establishes that the pattern generalises to
models with explicit reasoning procedures, which may well perform the
dominance calculation the present models appear to skip. Testing scale
invariance on reasoning models is the single most valuable extension of this
work, and we regard the present results as establishing the diagnostic rather
than settling the question.

The horizon confound remains. The Gemini arm knew the round count and the
others did not, so the Gemini column mixes model identity with horizon
knowledge. We have argued that this works against our conclusion in the one
place it matters --- the arm that knew the horizon warms up rather than
unravelling --- but it would be better to cross horizon explicitly.

The objective-framing inconsistency in the multilingual templates is inherited
from a widely used benchmark and, as reported above, absorbs most of one
model's apparent language effect. Our framing-controlled analysis rests on
three languages rather than five, which reduces power; the two null results
(Gemini, GPT-4o) should be read with that in mind. We would encourage users
of multilingual game-theoretic benchmarks to verify objective statements
across translations before treating language as a clean factor.

Finally, ten rounds is short. It under-identifies the memory-one vocabulary,
as reported, and it compresses any endgame effect into a small number of
rounds. A longer horizon would sharpen both the strategy read-out and the
unravelling test.

\subsection{Conclusion}

Four frontier language models playing 2{,}400 iterated prisoner's dilemmas
fail invariances that any game-theoretic description of them would require.
Rescaling payoffs, which cannot change the game, moves cooperation by up to
0.24. Changing the prompt language while holding the objective framing fixed
moves it by up to 0.15. Assigning a persona, which carries no strategic
information, moves it by up to 0.35. Against these, the entire crossed design
explains 18.7\% of the variance in dyad-level cooperation, and the play that
results is not generated by any memory-one rule: three of four models end
more cooperative than they start, three of four cooperate more after being
exploited than after exploiting, and in half to seven-eighths of agent-games
no rule in the class fits at all.

We therefore propose a minimal standard for this literature. A cooperation
rate reported for a language model should be accompanied by its sensitivity
to payoff scale and to prompt language, and a strategy label should be
accompanied by the fraction of games in which that label was deductively
established. These are cheap to compute and they change what the numbers
mean. Until they are routine, the field is at risk of building an
evolutionary game theory of artificial agents on quantities that are
properties of prompts rather than of agents.
