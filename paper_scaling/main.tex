\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Multiplying every payoff by a positive constant leaves the outcome ordering,
the dominant action, the equilibrium set and the replicator dynamics
untouched, so for any agent playing the game the predicted effect is zero by
theorem rather than by convention. We apply it to seven language models in the
iterated prisoner's dilemma, across six payoff scales spanning five orders of
magnitude, five prompt languages and 12{,}000 agent-games. Cooperation moves
in six of the seven, by up to 0.32, and not monotonically: lowest where the
cells print as decimals, highest where they print as short integers,
intermediate where they print as thousands. A three-level factor built from
how the numbers are written beats any polynomial in the log scale and matches
one saturated in it. The effect is already open on the first move, before any
history exists. It reaches the strategy label selectively, moving the
unconditional rules but not the reciprocal ones or the coordination structure,
and shifts how far a persona instruction is obeyed in every model. Two
frontier models change places in the cooperation ranking, so a cooperation
rate reported for a language model is a statement about one arbitrary choice
of units, and should be published with its scale sensitivity.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} machine behaviour; large language models; iterated
prisoner's dilemma; payoff scaling; utility invariance; benchmark validity

\section{Introduction}

Language models are now deployed as \emph{agents} that negotiate, coordinate
and decide on behalf of their
users~\citep{park2023generative,dafoe2021cooperative}, and a fast-growing
literature measures how they behave when they do. The instrument of choice is
the classical game, and the prisoner's dilemma above all, because its strategy
space is mapped and its predictions are
sharp~\citep{axelrod1981evolution,axelrod1984evolution,nowak1992tit,nowak2006five,press2012iterated}.
Studies in this vein report cooperation rates, departures from Nash play,
model-specific signatures and archetypes recovered from long
games~\citep{akata2023playing,phelps2023investigating,fontana2024nicer,mei2024turing,lore2024strategic},
and the numbers they produce increasingly feed models of mixed human-machine
populations~\citep{paiva2018engineering,santos2018social,rahwan2019machine}.

Every one of those measurements is made at a particular payoff matrix. The
matrix is written down once, in whatever units the experimenter chose, and the
cooperation rate is then reported as a property of the model. The choice of
units is not usually stated as a design decision at all, because in the theory
it is not one. A payoff matrix is a von Neumann--Morgenstern utility, and such
a utility is defined only up to a positive affine
transformation~\citep{vonneumann1944theory,luce1957games}. Writing the same
dilemma with cells ten times larger changes no preference, no best reply, no
equilibrium and no replicator trajectory. Under the theory the two matrices are
not two games written differently. They are one game.

That makes a positive rescaling the sharpest possible probe of whether a
language model is playing the game it was shown. Most manipulations one might
apply to a game-theoretic prompt have arguable effects: a change of framing
carries new words, a change of persona carries new information about the agent,
a change of language carries a different training distribution. A rescaling
carries none of these. It changes the numerals and nothing else. If behaviour
is a function of the game, the effect is exactly zero, and the null needs no
defending because it is a theorem. If behaviour moves, then whatever the model
is responding to is not the game.

This paper takes the rescaling itself as its object of study. We apply it
across five orders of magnitude to seven models in the iterated prisoner's
dilemma, crossed with five prompt languages and two persona instructions, and
ask four questions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Does the strategic behaviour of a language model survive multiplying every
      payoff by a positive constant, and by how much does it fail to?
\item If it moves, what does it move with? A rescaling changes the magnitude of
      the numbers and the way they are printed at the same time. These are
      different functions of $\lam$, and the data can tell them apart.
\item Where in the game does the effect enter: at the opening move, before any
      history exists, or through the course of play?
\item What else does the scale carry with it, and what does it leave alone?
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions are the following. \textbf{(i)} We report a response curve
over six payoff scales from $10^{-2}$ to $10^{3}$, and it is not monotone: it
rises, plateaus and falls (\S\ref{sec:response}). \textbf{(ii)} We show that
the response is better described by a three-level factor built from how the
cells are \emph{printed} than by any polynomial in $\log_{10}\lam$, and no
worse than a factor saturated in $\lam$ itself, at three fewer degrees of
freedom (\S\ref{sec:notation}). \textbf{(iii)} We show that the effect is
already open on the opening move, and that what moves is the level of
cooperation and not the coordination structure of play, whose miscoordination
rate is flat across all six scales (\S\ref{sec:opening}--\S\ref{sec:invariant}).
\textbf{(iv)} We show that the payoff scale shifts how far a persona
instruction is obeyed, in the same direction in every one of the seven models,
while leaving the sign of that effect a property of the model; and that the
scale interacts with the prompt language in both arms
(\S\ref{sec:gating}--\S\ref{sec:language}). \textbf{(v)} We show that the
strategy a read-out assigns to a run of play moves with the scale in all seven
models, and that it moves on the unconditional rules while the conditional
ones stay nearly still, which is the dissociation the coordination measure
records read at the level of the sequence (\S\ref{sec:labels}).
\textbf{(vi)} We show that the choice of $\lam$ reverses the cooperation
ranking of two frontier models, and propose a reporting standard that costs
one extra condition (\S\ref{sec:benchmark}, \S\ref{sec:standard}).

An earlier preprint by the present authors reported that the cooperation rate
of open-weight models varies with the payoff scale and with the prompt
language~\citep{selfpreprint2026scaling}. That report established the
phenomenon in one arm of the corpus. The present paper asks what the phenomenon
is a function of, and every contribution numbered above is new to it: the
frontier replication, the magnitude-against-notation comparison, the
localisation of the effect at the opening move, the persona result, the
coordination invariant, the strategy read-out, the ranking reversals and the
reporting standard. The
corpus is the same, re-analysed under the payoff polarity fixed in
\S\ref{sec:corpus}.

\section{Material and methods}

\subsection{The invariance under test}
\label{sec:invariance}

Let $u$ be a von Neumann--Morgenstern utility over the four outcomes of a
two-by-two game. The representation theorem says that $u$ and $a + b\,u$ with
$b > 0$ describe the same preferences~\citep{vonneumann1944theory,luce1957games}.
Every quantity a game theorist computes from the matrix is therefore invariant
under that map: the ordering of the outcomes, the best-reply correspondence,
the set of Nash equilibria, the dilemma indices greed $(T-R)/(T-S)$ and fear
$(P-S)/(T-S)$, and the replicator flow, which is invariant under $b$ up to a
rescaling of time and under $a$ exactly.

We manipulate the multiplicative half of that map, $u \mapsto \lam u$ with
$\lam > 0$, and we say so explicitly because the two halves are not equally
demanding. The additive half moves every cell by the same amount and can be
absorbed into a change of reference point; the multiplicative half changes the
number of digits in every cell and is therefore the half that a system reading
the matrix as text is most likely to notice. We do not test the additive half
here, and we note in \S\ref{sec:limits} what that leaves open.

Figure~\ref{fig:design}a states the stage game and the list of quantities that
$\lam$ cannot touch. Figure~\ref{fig:design}b states what it does touch, which
is the string the prompt prints.

\subsection{The game, the two arms and the crossing}
\label{sec:corpus}

FAIRGAME (Framework for AI Agents Bias Recognition using Game
Theory)~\citep{fairgame2025} provides the computational infrastructure.
Conditions are written in JSON; at run time the framework combines them with
language-specific prompt templates, simulates repeated normal-form games and
logs every round of play. We adopt the framework and its multilingual templates
as given, which keeps the wording of the manipulations out of our hands, and we
add the payoff scaling.

Agents played a symmetric two-player prisoner's dilemma. The templates state
the cell values as \emph{penalties}, that is, years of imprisonment in the
prisoner's-dilemma story, and instruct the agent to \emph{minimise} them, so a
smaller number is a better outcome and the ordering is $T < R < P < S$. That is
the penalty-space image of the usual $T > R > P > S$, and it makes Option A the
dominant, defecting action and Option B cooperation. We state the direction
explicitly because reversing it would reverse the sign of every result below.
The assignment is not a matter of interpretation: FAIRGAME logs the realised
payoff next to every action, and the assignment above reproduces the logged
payoff on all 264{,}000 rounds of the corpus while the opposite assignment
reproduces none of them.

The corpus has two arms (figure~\ref{fig:design}c). The \emph{open-weight arm}
is the one that carries the scale sweep. Three models, Gemma 3 12B, Llama 3.1
8B and Qwen3 8B, each met six payoff scales, $\lam \in \{0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100,
1000\}$, five prompt languages and four ordered persona pairings, over thirty
rounds. The \emph{frontier arm} replicates the design at a shorter horizon and
a narrower sweep. Four models, Claude 3.5 Haiku, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, GPT-4o
and Mistral Large, met three scales, $\lam \in \{0.1, 1, 10\}$, the same five
languages and the same four pairings, over ten rounds. Both arms are balanced
at exactly 80 agent-games in every model $\times$ language $\times$ scale cell,
with none missing. That gives 3{,}600 dyads and 7{,}200 agent-games in the
open-weight arm and 2{,}400 dyads and 4{,}800 agent-games in the frontier arm,
for 6{,}000 dyads, 12{,}000 agent-games and 264{,}000 binary decisions in all.

The base matrices differ between the arms. The frontier arm used $T = 0$, $R =
2$, $P = 6$, $S = 10$, which is greed 0.20 and fear 0.40 on the utility scale
$u = -\text{penalty}$; the open-weight arm used $P = 8$, which is greed 0.20
and fear 0.20. The two arms are therefore two dilemmas rather than one, and we
never pool them. Each is internally a single game shown at several scales,
which is the comparison every result below is made within.

On each round an agent received the full payoff table, its persona, the round
index and the complete history of both players' choices, and returned a single
token naming one of two options. Communication was disabled, so the only
channel between agents is the action history. Actions carry the labels
\texttt{OptionA} and \texttt{OptionB} in all five languages, which avoids the
extra meaning that ``cooperate'' and ``defect'' would bring; the templates never
say which label means cooperation, and the agent has to read it off the payoff
table. That is what makes the payoff numbers the load-bearing part of the
prompt, and it is why a change to them is the manipulation of interest.

Decoding used a fixed low output-token budget with reasoning traces disabled.
That setting is not a detail. A model that emits hidden reasoning can exhaust a
small budget inside the trace and return an incomplete string, and a harness
that then falls back to a default action turns that string into an apparent
cooperation rate of 1.0 that the parser has created rather than the model. We
logged parse-failure and fallback rates for every cell and required both to
stay below 0.02. Every dyad ran its full horizon and every decision resolved to
one of the two option tokens, so no cell loses games to a failure to parse.

\subsection{Notation regimes}
\label{sec:regimes}

Because $\lam$ changes the magnitude of the payoffs and the way they are
printed at the same time, we define the printed quantities in advance and let
the model comparison of \S\ref{sec:notation} arbitrate between them. For each
scale we take the four cell values as the template renders them and record
three features: whether any of them carries a decimal point, the mean number of
glyphs across the four, and a three-level \emph{notation regime} that
partitions the sweep into \emph{fractional} ($\lam < 1$, at least one cell
printed with a decimal point), \emph{unit} ($\lam \in \{1, 10\}$, largest cell
two or three digits) and \emph{large} ($\lam \geq 100$, largest cell four or
five digits). Figure~\ref{fig:design}b lists the six rows.

One property of that partition has to be stated before any of it is
interpreted. In this design $\lam < 1$ and ``the cell prints with a decimal
point'' are the same event, so a fractional-notation account and an account
based on sub-unit magnitude make identical predictions here and cannot be
separated. What
the design \emph{can} separate is either of them from a smooth function of
$\log_{10}\lam$, and that is the comparison we run. We return to the
experiment that would break the remaining confound in \S\ref{sec:limits}.

\subsection{The strategy read-out, and where its labels come from}
\label{sec:readout}

A cooperation rate averages over a game. The second summary the literature
reports, and the one that feeds evolutionary models, is the strategy assigned
to a whole run of play, so we measure that too. Because the assignment is made
by an instrument rather than observed, the instrument is described here in
full and its output is reported by provenance rather than as a single label
column.

The vocabulary is the four canonical memory-one rules: AllC, AllD,
tit-for-tat~\citep{axelrod1981evolution} and win-stay
lose-shift~\citep{nowak1993strategy}. The read-out runs in two stages. Every
one of the four prescribes an action as a function of the previous round's
joint outcome alone, so the first stage checks by enumeration whether a
trajectory is \emph{exactly} what a rule would have played, and returns the
set of rules that fit; no learning is involved and the answer is a proof. The
first stage has three outcomes: exactly one rule fits, and the label is
provable; several fit, in which case the run is recorded as \emph{ambiguous},
which happens whenever the realised history never discriminates them, as when
an agent cooperates throughout against a cooperator and is thereby AllC, TFT
and WSLS at once; or none fits. Where none fits, a second stage supplies the
nearest rule: a single-layer long short-term memory
network~\citep{hochreiter1997long} with a 24-dimensional embedding and 96
hidden units, trained on synthetic play generated from the same four rules at
zero and five per cent execution noise, returns a posterior over them and we
take its argmax. Separate networks are trained for the two horizons, and each
arm is read with the network matching its own.

Two properties of that pipeline bear on how its output should be read, and
both are reported rather than assumed away. The first is provenance. Over the
open-weight arm 22.1\% of agent-games carry a provable rule, 11.8\% are
ambiguous and 66.1\% are named by the network; over the frontier arm the
shares are 25.5\%, 8.3\% and 66.2\%. Two thirds of the labels are therefore
inferences and not deductions, and of those a further 16.5\% and 25.7\% of all
agent-games sit below a posterior of 0.90, a floor at which a read-out
designed to abstain would decline to answer at all. The second is validation.
On held-out synthetic play the pipeline recovers the generating rule with
accuracy 0.975 at ten rounds and 0.953 at thirty, against Bayes-optimal
single-label ceilings of 0.987 and 0.966, and where a rule fires uniquely the
label is right 99.5\% and 98.7\% of the time. The open-weight arm sits almost
exactly at that validated operating point; the frontier arm departs from the
canonical rules by more than the training distribution does, so its labels are
nearest neighbours more often. The electronic supplementary material, \S S1,
gives the full validation, including how the network degrades on noise levels
it never saw.

We therefore treat the strategy mix as a second, coarser dependent variable
rather than as ground truth, and we report every test on it twice: once over
the five categories the read-out emits, and once over the four named rules
alone. The distinction matters because \emph{ambiguous} is a statement about
how identifiable the play is and not about which strategy was played, so a
shift into or out of that category is a shift in nameability. Where the two
versions of a test disagree we say so.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{sec:stats}

All intervals are 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals over 2{,}000 resamples
that resample whole dyads~\citep{efron1993introduction}. The choice of unit
matters: rounds within a game are autocorrelated and the two agent-rows of a
dyad share a history, so a row-level resample would narrow the interval by
roughly a factor of $\sqrt{2}$.

Whether a factor moves a measure at all is answered against a permutation null
that shuffles the factor label across \emph{dyads}, so a dyad keeps both of its
agent-rows together and the null preserves the dependence the design creates.
We report the observed largest between-level gap, the permutation $P$ value
over 2{,}000 draws, and the null's 95th percentile, which doubles as the
smallest shift the test could have detected. For distributions over strategy
labels we use the largest total-variation distance between two levels, with the
same dyad-level null.

Whether the response is a function of the magnitude or of the notation is a
model-comparison question, and we answer it with the Bayesian information
criterion~\citep{schwarz1978estimating} over a ladder of specifications fitted
to identical rows. Every specification carries the same controls for model,
language, own persona and opponent persona, and they differ only in how $\lam$
enters: not at all; linearly, quadratically or cubically in $\log_{10}\lam$; as
the fractional flag; as the fractional flag plus the glyph count; as the
three-level notation regime; or saturated, as a free level per scale. The
saturated specification is the upper bound on fit that any function of $\lam$
can reach, so a description that matches it at fewer parameters is a
description that has found the right shape.

We do not correct for multiple comparisons across models, and we say so rather
than leave the point to be inferred. Each model is a separate system and is its
own family, so the tests within a manipulation are seven families of one rather
than one family of seven. The claims we draw are in any case about whether the
rescaling moves behaviour at all, not about which model moves most.

\subsection{Confounds we declare}
\label{sec:confounds}

Three features of the design are confounded with the arm, and one with a single
cell. The two arms differ in horizon (thirty rounds against ten), in whether
the round count was disclosed (it was in the open-weight arm, and in the
frontier arm only for Gemini), in sampling temperature, which follows each
provider's recommended default as in the FAIRGAME protocol, and in the base
matrix as recorded in \S\ref{sec:corpus}. All four are constant within a model,
so none of them can generate a within-model effect of $\lam$, which is what
every result below is. They do mean that no coverage or level statistic is
comparable across arms, and we compare none.

Separately, four dyads in the Claude $\times$ English $\times$ $\lam = 10$ cell
are logged with the payoffs of $\lam = 1$. We recovered the realised scale of
every game from the logged values rather than assuming it from the directory
name, which is how we found them; they are 8 agent-games out of 12{,}000, and
dropping them moves Claude's cooperation rate at $\lam = 10$ from 0.708 to
0.715. We retain them at their nominal scale and note that no conclusion turns
on them.

\subsection{A rescaling that cannot matter, and does}
\label{sec:response}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig2_response.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Cooperation against the payoff scale.}
(\textbf{a}) The open-weight arm over six scales. Bands are 95\% bootstrap
intervals over 2{,}000 resamples of whole dyads. The shaded strip marks the
unit regime. The curve is not monotone in any of the three models: it rises to

(\textbf{b}) The frontier arm over three scales, which sample only the rising

(\textbf{c}) The invariance test. Bars give the largest shift in cooperation

predicts is exactly zero. Six of the seven models exceed their null.}
\label{fig:response}
\end{figure}

Multiplying every payoff by a positive constant moves the cooperation rate
(figure~\ref{fig:response}). Pooled over the open-weight arm it runs 0.357
$\ci{0.332}{0.382}$ at $\lam = 0.01$, 0.384 $\ci{0.357}{0.410}$ at $0.1$,
0.573 $\ci{0.547}{0.599}$ at $1$, 0.567 $\ci{0.542}{0.592}$ at $10$, 0.476
$\ci{0.451}{0.503}$ at $100$ and 0.502 $\ci{0.477}{0.529}$ at $1000$. Pooled
over the frontier arm it runs 0.489 $\ci{0.467}{0.510}$, 0.633
$\ci{0.616}{0.651}$ and 0.631 $\ci{0.612}{0.649}$ across its three scales. The
same game, written with a different number of digits, is played differently.

Per model the largest shift between two scales is 0.316 for Gemma 3 12B, 0.267
for Qwen3 8B, 0.198 for Llama 3.1 8B, 0.240 for Claude 3.5 Haiku, 0.234 for
GPT-4o, 0.080 for Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and 0.036 for Mistral Large. All but
the last exceed a permutation null that shuffles the scale label across dyads:
$P < 0.001$ for the first five and $P = 0.0075$ for Gemini, against null 95th
percentiles between 0.047 and 0.121 (table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). Mistral
Large is the exception, at $P = 0.38$, and we report it as one: on this measure
its behaviour is invariant to the rescaling in the way the theory says it
should be. Its strategy mix is not, and we return to that in
\S\ref{sec:labels}.

The shape of the curve is the first substantive result, and it is only visible
because the open-weight arm sweeps five orders of magnitude. The response is
not monotone. In all three open-weight models cooperation rises from its
minimum in the fractional regime to a maximum at $\lam = 1$ or $\lam = 10$ and
then falls again by $\lam = 100$: Gemma from 0.345 to 0.661 and back to 0.512,
Qwen3 from 0.334 to 0.601 and back to 0.465, Llama from 0.300 to 0.499 and back
to 0.450. A three-scale window at
$\{0.1, 1, 10\}$, which is what the frontier arm and most published designs
sample, lies entirely on the rising limb and would support the conclusion that
larger payoffs produce more cooperation. Over the wider sweep that conclusion
is false.

\subsection{Magnitude or notation}
\label{sec:notation}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig3_notation.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{What the response is a function of.}
(\textbf{a}) Cooperation by notation regime, pooled within each arm. Whiskers
are 95\% dyad bootstrap intervals. The frontier arm has no large regime,
because its sweep stops at $\lam = 10$.
(\textbf{b}) The description ladder for the open-weight arm. Bars give the BIC
penalty each specification pays relative to the best one, with model, language
and both personas controlled in every row. A three-level factor built from how
the cells are printed beats every polynomial in $\log_{10}\lam$ and beats the

(\textbf{c}) Cooperation by model across the sweep, on an evenly spaced scale

every larger scale, so which of the two is the more cooperative model is a
statement about the units.}
\label{fig:notation}
\end{figure}

The response tracks the notation. Pooled over the open-weight arm, cooperation
in the fractional regime is 0.370 $\ci{0.352}{0.388}$, in the unit regime
0.570 $\ci{0.553}{0.588}$ and in the large regime 0.489
$\ci{0.472}{0.508}$ (figure~\ref{fig:notation}a); the same ordering holds separately
in all three models. In the frontier arm, which has no large regime,
cooperation is 0.489 $\ci{0.467}{0.510}$ in the fractional regime against
0.632 $\ci{0.619}{0.644}$ in the unit regime, and the ordering again holds
separately in every model.

The description ladder makes the point quantitatively
(figure~\ref{fig:notation}b, table~\ref{tab:ladder}). On the open-weight arm,
against the best specification, a model with no $\lam$ term at all pays 374 BIC
points, a linear term in $\log_{10}\lam$ pays 247, a quadratic pays 110 and a
cubic pays 118. A single binary feature, whether the cell prints with a decimal
point, pays 56, and adding the glyph count brings that to 16. The best
specification is the three-level notation regime, and the specification
saturated in $\lam$, which spends three more parameters and is the ceiling on
what any function of $\lam$ can fit, pays 20 points more than it. Two features
of how the numbers are written therefore capture what a free parameter per
scale captures, while a smooth function of the magnitude, at any of the three
orders we fitted, does not.

The frontier arm agrees where it can. With only two regimes present, the
fractional flag and the notation regime are the same one-parameter
specification there, and it beats the quadratic and the saturated model alike
by 8.4 BIC points, and the linear term by 53.7.

Per model (electronic supplementary material, table S3), five of the seven
prefer a notation description: Llama and Qwen3 in
the open-weight arm, by 258 and 149 BIC points over the quadratic, and Claude,
GPT-4o and Gemini in the frontier arm. The two exceptions run in opposite
directions and we report both, including the one that runs against us. Gemma
3 12B prefers a cubic in $\log_{10}\lam$, with the saturated specification 3.3
points behind it, the fractional flag plus glyph count 9.2 behind and the
notation regime 24.3 behind; for that model a smooth function of the magnitude
is the better description, and it is the single case in the corpus where the
notation account loses to a polynomial. Mistral Large prefers no $\lam$ term
at all, which is the correct description of a model whose cooperation rate did
not move.

\subsection{Which model looks more cooperative depends on the units}
\label{sec:benchmark}

The consequence for measurement is direct (figure~\ref{fig:notation}c). Within
the open-weight arm, the smallest scale swing inside a model, 0.198 for Llama,
is larger than the median spread between the three models at a fixed scale,
0.114. Which $\lam$ the experimenter wrote down therefore matters more, for the
number that comes out, than which of the three models was asked. The frontier
arm does not reproduce that ordering, because its models are further apart:
there the median between-model spread is 0.253 against a largest within-model
swing of 0.240.

Rankings reverse. Of the 45 model-pair by scale-pair comparisons available in
the open-weight arm, 8 change sign, and of the 18 available in the frontier arm,
2 do. Concretely, Qwen3 8B cooperates more than Gemma 3 12B at $\lam = 0.01$
(0.387 against 0.345) and less at every larger scale, reaching 0.574 against
0.661 at $\lam = 1$. In the frontier arm Mistral Large cooperates more than
Claude 3.5 Haiku at $\lam = 0.1$ (0.502 against 0.468) and less at $\lam = 1$
(0.538 against 0.700). A benchmark that fixes one scale reports one side of
these comparisons and has no way to signal that the other side exists.

\subsection{The effect is set at the opening move}
\label{sec:opening}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig4_where.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Where the effect enters, and what it leaves alone.}
(\textbf{a}) Cooperation by notation regime across the game. Solid lines are
the open-weight arm, dashed the frontier arm; the four positions are round one
and three equal blocks of the remaining rounds. The gap between the regimes is
open at round one, before any history exists.
(\textbf{b}) Opening-move cooperation against the payoff scale, one line per
model, colours as in figure~\ref{fig:response}.
(\textbf{c}) The largest shift across scales for six measures of the same
games, against the same permutation null. Dark bars are the open-weight arm,
pale the frontier arm; grey marks a measure whose shift does not clear its

agents fail to match does not.}
\label{fig:where}
\end{figure}

A cooperation rate is an average over a game, so a difference between two
scales could enter anywhere in it. It enters at the first move
(figure~\ref{fig:where}a,b). In the open-weight arm the gap between the
fractional and unit regimes is $-0.129$ on round one alone, against $-0.199$
over the whole game, so the opening move already carries 65\% of it. In the
frontier arm the round-one gap is $-0.199$ against $-0.142$ over the whole
game: there the effect is \emph{larger} at the opening than on average, and
shrinks as play proceeds, from $-0.199$ on round one to $-0.095$ over rounds
seven to ten.

Round one is the round on which no history exists. The agent has seen the
payoff table, its persona and the round index, and nothing else. Whatever the
rescaling does, therefore, it does to the prior over actions that the prompt
induces, and not to anything the agent learns from its opponent. The two arms
then diverge in what happens afterwards, which we report as a difference rather
than a finding: over thirty rounds the open-weight gap widens slightly, from
$-0.129$ to $-0.210$, while over ten rounds the frontier gap closes by half.
Neither trajectory creates the effect, and the shorter horizon does not
manufacture it.

\subsection{What the rescaling does not move}
\label{sec:invariant}

A corpus in which every measure moves is a corpus one should be suspicious of.
This one is not of that kind (figure~\ref{fig:where}c). Measured on the same
games and against the same dyad-level null, the level of cooperation moves in
both arms, and so do mutual cooperation, mutual defection, efficiency and the
opening move, all at $P < 0.001$. The rate of miscoordination, that is, the
share of rounds in which the two agents chose differently, does not: it swings
by 0.039 across six scales in the open-weight arm ($P = 0.14$, null 95th
percentile 0.047) and by 0.015 across three in the frontier arm ($P = 0.56$,
null 0.035), while mutual cooperation over the same rows swings by 0.198 and
0.139.

The distinction is worth stating plainly. Scaling the payoffs moves how much
the agents cooperate and leaves untouched how well they coordinate. Two agents
at $\lam = 0.01$ and two at $\lam = 1$ agree with each other equally often;
what differs is what they agree on. The asymmetry between the two positions in
a dyad behaves the same way in the open-weight arm, where the first-listed
agent cooperates between 0.054 and 0.099 less than the second at every one of
the six scales, with no trend across them.

\subsection{The scale shifts how far the persona instruction is obeyed}
\label{sec:gating}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig5_gating.pdf}

(\textbf{a}) The effect of being told to be cooperative rather than selfish,

\label{fig:gating}
\end{figure}

Each agent received a system-prompt persona, either \emph{cooperative} or
\emph{selfish}. A persona is not irrelevant by theorem, but it carries no
information about the game: the four pairings present identical payoff
matrices, and an agent was never told its opponent's persona. What the persona
does carry is an instruction, and how far that instruction is obeyed turns out
to depend on the payoff scale (figure~\ref{fig:gating}a,
table~\ref{tab:persona}).

The result has to be stated at the level of the individual model, because the
pooled average is misleading here in a way worth setting out. Pooled over the
open-weight arm the effect of the cooperative persona is $-0.095$
$\ci{-0.139}{-0.051}$ at $\lam = 0.01$, turns positive at $+0.113$
$\ci{0.068}{0.159}$ at $\lam = 10$ and collapses to $-0.003$
$\ci{-0.049}{0.044}$ at $1000$; the frontier pooled curve runs $-0.461$
$\ci{-0.494}{-0.429}$, $+0.030$ $\ci{-0.005}{0.065}$ and $+0.073$
$\ci{0.039}{0.109}$. Read alone, those two curves say that the instruction is
inverted in the fractional regime, obeyed in the unit regime and inert in the
large one. That reading does not survive disaggregation. In five of the seven
models the effect keeps one sign across the entire sweep: it is positive at
every scale for Gemma 3 12B and Llama 3.1 8B and negative at every scale for
Qwen3 8B, Claude 3.5 Haiku and Mistral Large. Every one of those 21 estimates
excludes zero except Gemma at $\lam = 0.01$, and every one of the five is
signed consistently once the scales are pooled within a regime
(table~\ref{tab:persona}). Only GPT-4o and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite cross zero. The pooled sign change is produced by three open-weight
models whose constant-sign effects change in magnitude, not by any model
reversing: Qwen3's inversion weakens from $-0.442$ to $-0.250$ while Gemma's
compliance strengthens from $+0.138$ to $+0.382$. The apparent null in the
large regime is the same arithmetic, since at $\lam = 100$ the three
open-weight effects are $+0.152$, $+0.101$ and $-0.314$ and each of the three
excludes zero. Consistent with that, the three-way interaction between model,
regime and persona is itself significant, at $F(3, \cdot) = 50.8$,
$P = 2.7\times10^{-32}$ in the frontier arm and $F(4, \cdot) = 7.6$,
$P = 4.2\times10^{-6}$ in the open-weight arm, so the pooled two-way
interaction is not a summary of a pattern the models share.

What the models do share is the direction of the shift. Measured as a
difference in differences with its own dyad bootstrap, the persona effect
moves toward compliance across the fractional-to-unit boundary in every one of
the seven, by $+0.244$, $+0.052$ and $+0.192$ in the open-weight arm and
$+0.256$, $+0.691$, $+0.707$ and $+0.396$ in the frontier arm, and all seven
intervals exclude zero. Where the sweep continues into the large regime all
three open-weight models move back the other way, by $-0.205$, $-0.049$ and
$-0.058$, the first two of which exclude zero. The persona effect therefore
follows the same non-monotone course as the cooperation rate itself, rising
into the unit regime and falling out of it, in seven of seven models and both
arms.

The two statements have to be kept apart, and separating them is what the
result is. \emph{Whether} a model obeys a cooperative persona at all is a
property of that model, stable across five orders of magnitude in the payoff
scale, and nothing in this corpus suggests the units decide it. \emph{How
strongly} it obeys is not a property of the model: it is shifted, in a common
direction and by as much as 0.71, by a change to the payoff matrix that
carries no instruction, no information about the task, and no game-theoretic
content whatever. For two of the seven models that shift is large enough to
carry the effect across zero, which is how the same system prompt comes to
raise cooperation at one scale and lower it at another in the same model.

\subsection{Language and objective framing}
\label{sec:language}

The prompt language interacts with the scale as well
(figure~\ref{fig:gating}b). In the open-weight arm the spread between the five
languages ranges from 0.162 to 0.427 depending on which scale it is measured
at, and the scale swing ranges from 0.191 to 0.383 depending on which language
it is measured in (electronic supplementary material, table S8); the
interaction between the notation regime and language is
$F(8, \cdot) = 19.4$, $P = 3.5 \times 10^{-29}$. The frontier arm gives the
same result at a smaller size, $F(4, \cdot) = 7.9$, $P = 2.3 \times 10^{-6}$.
The
languages do not move in parallel: cooperation in Arabic falls from 0.423 at
$\lam = 10$ to 0.232 at $\lam = 1000$ while cooperation in Vietnamese rises
from 0.565 to 0.659 over the same two scales.

One feature of the FAIRGAME templates makes a partial mechanism test available,
and we use it rather than treat it only as a limitation. The templates do not
state the objective identically in every language: English, French and
Vietnamese tell the agent to minimise a penalty, while Chinese and Arabic tell
it to maximise a reward, with the cell values still described as penalties.
Language is therefore confounded with objective framing in this corpus, which
means the five-language contrast is an omnibus test and we report it as one.

The confound is informative here because a natural account of the scale effect
predicts something specific about it. If models were reading the displayed
numbers as rewards to be maximised rather than penalties to be minimised, and
if raising $\lam$ made the largest attainable number easier to notice, then the
direction of the scale effect should differ between the two framings. It does
not. Under both framings the open-weight response has the same non-monotone
shape, rising from 0.302 to 0.494 and falling to 0.386 under the
maximise-framed languages, and rising from 0.393 to 0.640 and falling to 0.579
under the minimise-framed ones. Framing shifts the level of cooperation, by
0.136 in the open-weight arm and by only $-0.007$ in the frontier arm, and does
not reverse or reshape the response to $\lam$. The scale effect is therefore
not a reward-reading effect, or at least not only one.

\subsection{The strategy label moves, and it moves selectively}
\label{sec:labels}

Cooperation rate is one summary. A second, and the one that feeds evolutionary
models of mixed populations, is the strategy a read-out assigns to a whole run
of play. We pass every agent-game through the hybrid read-out of
\S\ref{sec:readout}, which proves a label from the four canonical memory-one
rules where one of them exactly reproduces the run, records the run as
ambiguous where several do, and otherwise takes the argmax of an LSTM trained
on synthetic play from the same four. The mix over those five categories is
the dependent variable (figure~\ref{fig:gating}c).

It moves with the scale in all seven models. The largest total-variation
distance between the mixes at two scales is 0.375 for Gemma, 0.388 for Llama
and 0.278 for Qwen3, against dyad-level permutation nulls of 0.143, 0.115 and
0.123; and 0.263 for Claude, 0.338 for Gemini, 0.325 for GPT-4o and 0.125 for
Mistral Large, against nulls of 0.115, 0.120, 0.103 and 0.098. Every one of the
seven exceeds its null, at $P < 0.001$ for six and $P = 0.006$ for Mistral
Large. Restricting the vocabulary to the four named rules, so that movement
into and out of the ambiguous category cannot contribute, leaves all seven
significant, at 0.205, 0.388, 0.275, 0.245, 0.304, 0.296 and 0.127 in the same
order. One model changes materially under that restriction and we flag it:
Gemma's shift falls from 0.375 to 0.205, because almost half of its
five-category distance is movement into the ambiguous bucket rather than
between strategies. For the other six the two versions agree to within 0.04.

The shape of the movement is the substantive part, and a single distance
cannot show it (figure~\ref{fig:gating}c, table~\ref{tab:strategy}). Resolved
into one curve per label, the open-weight AllD share runs 0.531, 0.549, 0.326,
0.332, 0.428 and 0.403 across the six scales and the AllC share runs 0.216,
0.193, 0.335, 0.354, 0.279 and 0.284. These are the cooperation curve of
figure~\ref{fig:response}a and its mirror image: the same rise into the unit
regime, the same plateau across a full order of magnitude, the same fall above
$\lam = 100$. The frontier arm reproduces the rising limb, AllD falling from
0.388 to 0.185 and AllC rising from 0.303 to 0.393 between $\lam = 0.1$ and
$\lam = 1$. The notation result therefore holds on a read-out that consumes
the whole sequence, and not only on an average over rounds.

The two conditional rules behave differently, and the contrast is the point.
Over the open-weight sweep the TFT share moves by 0.049 and the WSLS share by
0.020, against the 0.223 of AllD and the 0.161 of AllC; measured against the
same dyad-level null, TFT clears it narrowly ($P = 0.0015$, null 0.038) and
WSLS does not clear it at all ($P = 0.36$, null 0.029). The frontier arm gives
the same ordering, with TFT at 0.050 and WSLS at 0.067 against AllD's 0.203.
What the payoff scale moves is how often an agent is assigned an
\emph{unconditional} rule, and which one; how often it is assigned a rule that
responds to its opponent barely moves. That is the invariant of
\S\ref{sec:invariant} seen at the level of the sequence rather than the round:
reciprocal play is what produces coordination, and reciprocal play is what the
rescaling leaves alone.

Two further observations belong here because they qualify what the read-out
can be asked to support. First, Mistral Large is the model whose cooperation
rate did not move, and its strategy mix does, at $P = 0.006$ over the five
categories and $P = 0.004$ over the four named rules. A model can cooperate at the same rate under two scales and
still be assigned a different distribution over strategies, because the rate
is an average and the label is a claim about the sequence; invariance on one
summary is not invariance on the other. Second, the read-out's own coverage is
not constant across the sweep. The share of open-weight agent-games carrying a
provable rule falls from 0.284 at $\lam = 0.01$ to 0.172 at $\lam = 10$ and
recovers to 0.207 at $\lam = 1000$, while the ambiguous share rises from 0.055
to 0.173; in the frontier arm provable coverage falls from 0.360 to 0.190
across three scales and the share below the posterior floor rises from 0.219
to 0.290. Play is at its least exactly nameable at the scales where
cooperation is highest, which is a property of the instrument as much as of
the behaviour, and it is a further reason to read the named-vocabulary column
of table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} alongside the five-category one rather than
instead of it.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{A cooperation rate is a statement about one choice of units}

The central result is that the strategic behaviour of a language model is not
invariant to the one manipulation whose null is a theorem. Six of seven models
move, by up to 0.32 in cooperation rate and up to 0.39 in total variation over
strategy labels, when every payoff is multiplied by a positive constant. This
is not a framing effect in the sense the term usually carries, because no
description changed. The words of the prompt, the ordering of the options, the
identity of the dominant action and the structure of the dilemma are all
identical between two conditions that differ by a factor of ten.

The consequence for measurement is that a published cooperation rate for a
language model is conditional on a design choice that the theory says is not a
choice, and that is therefore almost never reported. The size of that
conditioning is not small. In the open-weight arm the smallest within-model
swing across the sweep exceeds the median spread between the three models at a
fixed scale, and eight of forty-five model-pair comparisons change sign
somewhere in the sweep. Two frontier models change places between $\lam = 0.1$
and $\lam = 1$. A field that compares models on cooperation is comparing them
at whichever scale each paper happened to write down.

\subsection{Notation, and what the account can and cannot claim}

The response is not a smooth function of the magnitude. A cubic in
$\log_{10}\lam$ fits worse than a single binary feature of the printed string,
and a two-parameter description built from how the cells are written matches
what a free parameter per scale achieves. Read with the shape of the curve,
which rises, plateaus and falls, the picture is a step function over notation
regimes rather than a dose-response in the payoffs.

We are explicit about the limit of that claim. In this design $\lam < 1$ and
``the cells print with a decimal point'' are the same event, so the results are
equally consistent with a notation account and with an account under which
sub-unit magnitudes are simply treated as small stakes. The evidence that
favours notation is indirect and comes from three directions. First, the
response is flat between $\lam = 1$ and $\lam = 10$, a full order of magnitude,
in both arms, which a stake-size account has to explain away. Second, the
response turns down again above $\lam = 100$, so it is not monotone in stakes
in either direction. Third, most of the effect is already there on the opening
move, which is where a reading of the prompt lives and not where an evaluation
of accumulated stakes could be.

The decisive experiment is one restatement away and we did not run it. Print
the $\lam = 1$ matrix as ``0.0, 2.0, 8.0, 10.0'' rather than ``0, 2, 8, 10'',
which holds the magnitude fixed and changes only the notation, and print the
$\lam = 0.1$ matrix in a unit where the cells are integers, which holds the
notation fixed and changes only the magnitude. The two cells of that design
separate the accounts completely. We regard the present results as establishing
that the response is a step function over how the matrix is written, and as
leaving open which feature of the writing does the work.

\subsection{An instruction is only as good as the numbers next to it}

The persona result is the one we would put first in front of anyone building
agent systems, and it needs stating in two parts because the two carry
different weight.

The part that is common to every model is a shift. The strength with which a
model follows a cooperative persona moves toward compliance when the payoffs
next to that instruction are written as short integers rather than decimals,
in all seven models, by between 0.05 and 0.71, and moves back away from
compliance when they are written as thousands, in all three models whose sweep
goes that far. Nothing about the instruction changed, nothing about the task
changed, and nothing a game theorist can compute from the matrix changed.
Instruction-following is normally treated as a property of a model, measured
and reported as such; what this says is that it is a property of a model and
its numeric context jointly, and that the relevant part of the context can be
a part carrying no instruction and no information about the task beyond its
units. An evaluation of whether a model follows a behavioural instruction, run
at one payoff scale, does not transfer to another scale of the same task.

The part that is \emph{not} common to every model is the sign, and we were
careful in \S\ref{sec:gating} not to claim otherwise, because the pooled
average invites it. Two of the seven models obey a cooperative persona at one
payoff scale and invert it at another; the other five keep whatever
disposition they have across five orders of magnitude. Whether a system prompt
is followed or reversed therefore remains a model-level fact worth measuring
once. How much it is followed is not, and cannot be reported without the scale
it was measured at. A practitioner should read the first as a property to
select a model on, and the second as a reason to test the prompt at the units
the deployment will actually use.

\subsection{Consequences for models of hybrid populations}

Work that models populations in which artificial and human agents
interact~\citep{paiva2018engineering,santos2018social,dafoe2021cooperative},
and the evolutionary-game machinery it draws
on~\citep{szabo2007evolutionary,perc2017statistical}, assigns agents
strategies from a known family and computes the resulting dynamics. Three of
our results bear on that programme directly. First, the assignment is not
stable under a transformation the dynamics cannot see: the replicator equation
is identical at $\lam$ and $10\lam$, and the strategy mix we would feed into it
differs by up to 0.39 in total variation, or 0.39 over the four named rules
alone. Two population models that a game theorist would call the same model
therefore predict differently about the same agent.

Second, the instability sits in exactly the coordinate such models are most
sensitive to. What moves is the split between AllC and AllD, by 0.16 and 0.22
over the open-weight sweep, and the seeded proportion of unconditional
cooperators and defectors is what sets whether a replicator trajectory runs to
fixation or to coexistence. Third, and cutting the other way, the reciprocal
strategies are close to invariant: TFT moves by 0.05 and WSLS by 0.02, and
WSLS does not clear its null at all. Since it is the reciprocal share that
governs whether cooperation can be sustained against invasion at
all~\citep{axelrod1981evolution,nowak1993strategy}, the part of the mix that
carries the qualitative behaviour of the dynamics is the part the rescaling
leaves alone, and the part it moves is the initial condition. That is a
narrower problem than the raw total-variation number suggests, and a more
tractable one.

The practical implication is not that the programme is unworkable. It is that a
population model built on measured LLM strategies inherits a free parameter
that the theory does not contain, that the parameter enters through the
initial mix rather than through the reciprocal core, and that it should be
swept rather than fixed.

\subsection{Relation to the prompt-sensitivity and framing literatures}

That language model outputs move with features of the prompt that carry no
task content is established: formatting choices, separators and option order
all shift
accuracy~\citep{sclar2024quantifying,lu2022fantastically}, and personas shift
behaviour in ways that outrun what the persona
states~\citep{deshpande2023toxicity,salewski2023context}. Our contribution is
not that a spurious feature matters, but that the feature here is one the
relevant theory has already declared to be spurious with a proof. In an
accuracy benchmark, the claim that a separator should not matter is an
intuition. In a game, the claim that units should not matter is the
representation theorem, and that is what makes the size of the failure
interpretable rather than merely surprising.

The relation to the human framing literature runs the other
way~\citep{tversky1981framing,kahneman1979prospect}. Human framing effects are
produced by re-describing the same outcomes, and they map to a compact set of
psychological mechanisms: reference points, loss aversion, the salience of a
gain or a loss. Nothing is re-described here. Within an arm the two conditions
differ by a multiplicative constant, which is the one change that prospect
theory and expected utility theory agree in treating as inert once the units of
the value function are fixed. Our closest neighbour in the LLM literature is
the finding of~\citet{lore2024strategic} that contextual framing carries weight
comparable to game structure; we document a sensitivity one step further out,
to a change that is not a framing at all.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Six limitations bound these conclusions.

The notation and magnitude accounts are not separated, for the reason given in
\S\ref{sec:regimes}, and the experiment that would separate them is stated
above. This bounds the interpretation of \S\ref{sec:notation} and not the
result of \S\ref{sec:response}, which needs no account of the mechanism to
stand.

We test the multiplicative half of the affine invariance and not the additive
half. Adding a constant to every cell is the weaker of the two demands, since
it does not change the number of digits, and we would expect a smaller effect;
but it has a cleaner interpretation, because it moves the reference point
without touching the notation, and it is the natural companion experiment.

The two arms differ in horizon, in horizon disclosure, in decoding temperature
and in the base matrix, so nothing is comparable across them and we compare
nothing. The replication we claim is of the direction and the shape of the
response, both of which are measured within an arm and within a model.

The sweep is coarse. Six scales at decade spacing locate the maximum only to
within a decade, and the three-level regime partition is a coarse summary of a
response that a denser sweep might resolve differently. The claim that a smooth
function of $\log_{10}\lam$ is the wrong shape does not depend on the spacing;
the claim that three regimes is the right partition does.

The strategy result rests on an instrument, and the instrument is imperfect in
a way that is worth naming rather than absorbing. Two thirds of the labels are
supplied by a learned classifier rather than by a proof, and a sixth to a
quarter of all agent-games sit below the posterior floor at which a read-out
designed to abstain would decline to answer (\S\ref{sec:readout}). We have
reported every test on the four named rules as well as on all five categories,
and the conclusions of \S\ref{sec:labels} hold on both; but the exact-rule
coverage of the frontier arm is lower than the classifier's training
distribution, so the frontier labels in particular are nearest neighbours more
often than they are recognitions. What would remove the caveat is an
abstaining read-out, reported with its coverage, rather than a larger
vocabulary.

Finally, the model set is seven, none of them with an explicit reasoning
procedure, and reasoning traces were disabled by design so that the parser
could not manufacture cooperation. Whether a model that reasons at inference
time recovers the invariance is the single most valuable extension, and it is
the one experiment whose result we would not want to predict.

\subsection{A reporting standard, and what it costs}
\label{sec:standard}

The remedy is cheap, and it follows from the first result alone. Any
measurement of strategic behaviour in a language model should report the scale
at which the payoff matrix was written, and should report the measurement at
more than one scale. One decade of separation is enough to detect the effect
(electronic supplementary material, table S9).
Taking the single contrast $\lam = 0.1$ against $\lam = 1$, and holding it to
the null of the full sweep, which is conservative because a two-level design
generates a smaller one, the gap is 0.232, 0.226, 0.080 and 0.036 for the four
frontier models and 0.145, 0.182 and 0.240 for the three open-weight ones,
against nulls of 0.058, 0.062, 0.062, 0.065, 0.121, 0.047 and 0.095. Six of
the seven clear it, and the one that does not is the model that does not move
over the whole sweep either. Three conditions spanning two decades additionally
recover the shape. The cost is a factor of two or three in sampling on a
quantity that is otherwise reported as though it were a constant of the model.

Concretely, we propose that a cooperation rate be published alongside its
\emph{scale swing}, the largest difference between two scales, and its
permutation null. On this corpus those pairs are 0.316 against 0.121, 0.198
against 0.047, 0.267 against 0.095, 0.240 against 0.058, 0.080 against 0.062,
0.234 against 0.062 and 0.036 against 0.065. Six of the seven exceed the null,
and the seventh is a genuine and interesting negative that the current practice
of reporting one number cannot express.

\subsection{Conclusion}

Seven language models played 6{,}000 iterated prisoner's dilemmas in which the
only manipulation of the game was to multiply every payoff by a positive
constant, a transformation whose effect on any theory of the game is exactly
zero. Cooperation moved in six of the seven, by up to 0.32, and the strategy
mix moved in all seven. The response is not monotone in the payoff magnitude,
and it is better described by how the numbers are printed than by any
polynomial in their logarithm. It is already open on the opening move, so it
is a prior the notation induces and not something learned from play. It shifts
how far a persona instruction is obeyed, in the same direction in every model,
and it interacts with the prompt language. What it leaves alone is reciprocity:
how well the two agents coordinate, and how often either is playing a rule
that responds to the other.

The reading we draw is not that these models fail to be rational agents, which
would be a small claim about a well-known fact. It is that the quantity a
benchmark reports is jointly determined by the game and by an arbitrary choice
of units that the theory promised was free. Until that choice is swept rather
than fixed, a cooperation rate attributed to a model is attributed too widely.
