\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Multiplying every payoff in a game by a positive constant leaves preferences,
best replies, equilibria and replicator dynamics untouched, so for an agent
playing the game the predicted effect of that rescaling is zero by theorem
rather than by convention. We use it as an instrument. Five frontier language
models played an iterated prisoner's dilemma across ten payoff scales spanning
five orders of magnitude, crossed with five prompt languages and two persona instructions. The grid is complete and exactly balanced:
10{,}000 dyads, 20{,}000 agent-games and 200{,}000 decisions. Cooperation moves in every model, by up to
0.243 \ci{0.200}{0.290}, and the movement has a different shape in each one, so
the average over models reports a 0.048 swing where the typical model shows
0.136. In a variance decomposition the scale has no significant main effect: $0.7\%$
of cell variance, $p=0.36$. Its interactions with model ($6.5\%$) and with
prompt language ($3.8\%$) are both significant, which is what a pooled
benchmark is built to miss. The scale also gates the persona
instruction. Where every payoff printed is at most 1, the effect of telling a
model it is cooperative rather than selfish reverses sign in three models of
five, shifting by 0.680 \ci{0.625}{0.736} in the largest case. Reading the
transcripts with a hybrid rule-base and LSTM classifier shows the change is not only in how often the models cooperate but
in what they play. The share of agent-games matched exactly by a canonical
memory-one rule falls from $31.4\%$
to $19.5\%$ as the payoffs grow, and among the labels the classifier assigns,
the mix moves from unconditional towards reciprocal strategies. The effect is already present on the opening move,
before any history exists, in four models of five. A cooperation rate reported
for a language model is a statement about one arbitrary choice of units, and
should be published with its scale sensitivity.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} machine behaviour; large language models; iterated
prisoner's dilemma; payoff scaling; utility invariance; benchmark validity

\section{Introduction}

Language models are increasingly deployed as agents that negotiate, coordinate
and decide on behalf of their
users~\citep{park2023generative,dafoe2021cooperative}, and a fast-growing
literature measures how they behave when they do. The instrument of choice is
the classical game, and the prisoner's dilemma above all, because its strategy
space is mapped and its predictions are
sharp~\citep{axelrod1981evolution,axelrod1984evolution,nowak1992tit,nowak2006five,press2012iterated}.
Studies in this vein report cooperation rates, departures from Nash play, and
model-specific
signatures~\citep{akata2023playing,phelps2023investigating,fontana2024nicer,mei2024turing,lore2024strategic},
and those numbers increasingly feed models of mixed human-machine
populations~\citep{paiva2018engineering,santos2018social,rahwan2019machine}.

Every one of those measurements is made at a particular payoff matrix. The
matrix is written down once, in whatever units the experimenter chose, and the
cooperation rate is then reported as a property of the model. The choice of
units is not usually stated as a design decision at all, because in the theory
it is not one. A payoff matrix is a von Neumann--Morgenstern utility, and such
a utility is defined only up to a positive affine
transformation~\citep{vonneumann1944theory,luce1957games}. Writing the same
dilemma with cells ten times larger changes no preference, no best reply, no
equilibrium and no replicator trajectory. Under the theory the two matrices are
not two games written differently. They are one game.

This equivalence makes a positive rescaling the sharpest available probe of
whether a language model is playing the game it was shown. Most manipulations one might
apply to a game-theoretic prompt have arguable effects: a change of framing
carries new words~\citep{tversky1981framing,kahneman1979prospect}, a change of
persona carries new information about the agent~\citep{deshpande2023toxicity},
a change of language carries a different training distribution. A rescaling
carries none of these. It changes the numerals and nothing else. If behaviour
is a function of the game, the effect is exactly zero, and that null needs no
defending because it is a theorem. If behaviour moves, then whatever the model
is responding to is not the game.

Sensitivity to details that should not matter is already documented for
language models in other settings. Accuracy shifts with the order of in-context
examples~\citep{lu2022fantastically} and with semantically equivalent
rephrasings of a prompt~\citep{sclar2024quantifying}. Those findings establish
that surface form matters; they do not settle what happens inside a game,
because reordering examples or rewording an instruction has no theoretical
guarantee attached. Payoff rescaling does, and the guarantee is what makes the
measurement interpretable: any movement is a measured departure from a stated
invariance, not a difference between two conditions whose expected gap was
unknown to begin with.

This paper takes the rescaling as its object of study, and asks four questions
of it. Does strategic behaviour survive multiplying every payoff by a positive
constant, and by how much does it fail to? Does the answer depend on the
language the game is posed in, and on the persona the model is told to adopt?
Does the rescaling change only how often a model cooperates, or the strategy it
is playing? And where in the game does the effect enter: at the opening move,
before any history exists, or through the course of play?

We answer them on a complete and exactly balanced grid: five frontier models,
ten payoff scales from $\lam = 0.01$ to $\lam = 10^{3}$, five prompt languages,
two persona instructions in all four pairings, ten replicates and ten rounds.
The corpus holds 10{,}000 dyads, 20{,}000 agent-games and 200{,}000 recorded
decisions.
Four findings follow. First, cooperation moves in all five models, by up to
0.243, and the movement is significant against a permutation null that destroys
only the association with the scale. Second, the shape of the movement differs
between models to the point of anticorrelation, so pooling reports the effect
as roughly a third of its typical within-model size. In a variance
decomposition the scale main effect is not significant while its interactions
are.
Third, the scale gates the persona instruction, reversing its sign in three
models of five below the point where every printed payoff falls to 1 or less.
Fourth, the strategy itself changes: play becomes measurably less rule-governed
as the payoffs grow, and the composition moves from unconditional towards
reciprocal rules. The effect is already visible on the opening move.

We should be explicit about what this design can and cannot establish. It
measures departures from an invariance across a broad but finite grid; it does
not identify the mechanism inside the model that produces them, and with five
models it cannot support claims about model families or scale in parameters.
The corpus is one game, one prompt template family and one horizon, so the
generality of the specific shapes reported here is untested. What the design
does establish is that the departures exist, are large relative to their
sampling variability, and are structured rather than noisy.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Design}

Both agents in each dyad are given the standard prisoner's-dilemma prompt of
the FAIRGAME protocol~\citep{fairgame2025}, which states the payoffs as years
of imprisonment and instructs the agent to minimise them. At $\lam = 1$ the
matrix the agent is shown is $T=0$, $R=2$, $P=6$, $S=10$ in those penalty
units, so that the mutually cooperative cell carries the lowest symmetric
penalty and the unilateral defection cell carries none at all. Under this
framing OptionA is the dominant, defecting action and OptionB is the
cooperative one, and cooperation rates below are computed accordingly. The
manipulation multiplies all four cells by $\lam$, taking ten values from
$0.01$ to $10^{3}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}a). Nothing else in the prompt
changes.

The grid is crossed with five prompt languages (English, French, Vietnamese,
Chinese, Arabic), with a persona instruction that names each agent as
cooperative or selfish in all four pairings, and with ten replicates. Each game
runs ten rounds, with the horizon announced. Every one of the 250 cells of
the design contains exactly 200 dyads; the build refuses to proceed otherwise,
because a silently truncated cell is a failure this kind of corpus is prone to.
Overall cooperation is 0.565.

\subsection{Cooperation moves under a transformation that cannot matter}

Cooperation depends on the payoff scale in all five models
(Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}b, Table~\ref{tab:effect}). The range across the ten
scales runs from 0.072 \ci{0.060}{0.133} in Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite to 0.243
\ci{0.200}{0.290} in GPT-5.4 Nano, with Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite at 0.191
\ci{0.165}{0.240}. Confidence intervals resample whole dyads rather than
rounds, because the two agents of a dyad play each other and the ten rounds
within an agent-game are strongly autocorrelated.

Because the null here is a theorem, the appropriate test destroys the
association with the scale and leaves everything else in place. Shuffling the
scale label within language and persona, and recomputing the range 2{,}000
times, puts the observed value beyond every permuted one in all five models
($p = 0.0005$, the smallest value 2{,}000 draws can return). We also fit a per-model logistic regression of the round-level decision on the
scale as a ten-level factor, with language and both personas as covariates and
standard errors clustered on the dyad. It rejects the joint null on the nine
scale contrasts in every model ($\chi^2_9$ between 32.3 and 164.1, all
$p \leq 2\times10^{-4}$). The largest contrast against $\lam = 1$ ranges from
0.36 to 0.74 in log-odds.

The shapes are unlike one another. GPT-5.4 Nano rises steeply from 0.438 at
$\lam = 0.01$ and saturates near 0.65; Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite drifts downward
across the whole range; Claude Haiku 4.5 dips to a minimum at $\lam = 0.25$ and
recovers; Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite oscillates within a high band. Only the
direction of the smallest scales is at all shared, and even that is not
universal.

\subsection{Averaging across models reports the effect as almost absent}

The disagreement in shape has a direct consequence for how such measurements
are usually reported. Averaging the five model curves gives a pooled curve
whose range is 0.048, while the mean within-model range is 0.136, an
attenuation of $2.8$ times (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}c). The cause is visible in
the pairwise correlations between model curves: they average $-0.10$ and run
from $-0.72$ to $+0.73$, with six of the ten pairs negative. Curves that move
in opposite directions cancel when added.

A variance decomposition on the 250 cell means makes the same point in the
language a benchmark report would use. The model accounts for $56.5\%$ of the
variance across cells and the model-by-language interaction for $19.7\%$. The
payoff scale as a main effect accounts for $0.7\%$ and is not significant
($F_{9,144} = 1.10$, $p = 0.36$). Its interactions are: model by scale carries
$6.5\%$ ($p < 10^{-3}$) and language by scale $3.8\%$ ($p = 0.041$). A study
that fits a main effect for the payoff scale, pooling models and languages,
will therefore conclude correctly that there is no average effect, and will be
wrong about every individual model in the pool.

\subsection{The prompt language sets how large the effect is}

The prompt language changes both the level of cooperation and its sensitivity
to the scale (Fig.~\ref{fig:language}). Level differences are large and
model-specific, which is why the model-by-language interaction dominates the
decomposition above: Claude Haiku 4.5 cooperates 0.574 in Chinese and 0.234 in
Vietnamese, while GPT-5.4 Nano cooperates 0.798 in English and 0.458 in Arabic.

The sensitivity differences are the new observation. Within a single model, the
range of cooperation across the ten scales itself depends on the language:
0.136 in English against 0.314 in French for Claude Haiku 4.5, and 0.162 in
Chinese against 0.412 in English for Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite. Across all
model-language pairs the range runs from 0.080 to 0.449. The scale sensitivity
of a model is therefore not a single number even for that model; it is a
property of the model and the language together, and the language-by-scale
interaction in the decomposition is significant.

\subsection{The payoff scale gates the persona instruction}

Each agent is told it is either a cooperative or a selfish player, and the
difference between the cooperation rates of those two instructions measures how
much the persona takes hold. That difference depends on the payoff scale, and
the dependence is not gradual (Fig.~\ref{fig:persona}, Table~\ref{tab:persona}).

The natural boundary in this design is where the printed numbers stop being
sub-unit. With base payoffs $0, 2, 6, 10$, the largest number shown is
$10\lam$, so every payoff is at most 1 exactly when $\lam \leq 0.1$, which two
of the ten scales satisfy. Across that boundary the persona effect reverses
sign in three models of five. In Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite it runs from $-0.456$
below to $+0.224$ above, a shift of 0.680 \ci{0.625}{0.736}. In Claude Haiku
4.5 it runs from $-0.222$ to $+0.207$, a shift of 0.429 \ci{0.389}{0.471}, and
in GPT-5.4 Nano from $-0.135$ to $+0.018$, a shift of 0.153
\ci{0.105}{0.200}. Below the
boundary these models cooperate \emph{more} when told they are selfish. The two
models that do not reverse still move in the same direction, by 0.103
\ci{0.075}{0.131} and 0.233 \ci{0.210}{0.258}.

The boundary is a fact about magnitude and not about decimal notation, and the
grid separates the two. At $\lam = 0.25$ the payoffs are $0, 0.5, 1.5, 2.5$:
still written as decimals, but no longer all below 1. The persona effect at
that scale already has its supra-unit sign and size in all three reversing
models. Whatever the models are responding to, it tracks how large the numbers
are rather than whether a decimal point appears.

Qwen3 235B-A22B deserves separate comment because it inverts the persona
instruction at every scale, cooperating 0.072 under the cooperative persona
and 0.798 under the selfish one. The prisoner's dilemma story supports two
readings of what it means to cooperate, with one's partner or with the
authorities, and this model appears to resolve that ambiguity opposite to the
convention the payoff matrix encodes. The inversion is not our subject, but its
magnitude is scale-dependent in the same way as everything else here: it
attenuates monotonically from $-0.912$ to $-0.679$ as the payoffs grow.

\subsection{The scale changes which strategy is played}

A cooperation rate compresses a ten-round trajectory to one number, and two
models with the same rate can be playing different games. We therefore read
each agent-game against the four canonical memory-one strategies: AllC, AllD,
Tit-for-Tat and Win-Stay-Lose-Shift. The procedure is hybrid, with a rule base
doing the deducing and a learned sequence model only ranking what the rules
leave open (\S\ref{sec:methods}). The distinction matters for how the results
should be read, so the read-out reports three provenances separately: the
agent-games where exactly one rule fits the trajectory exactly, where several
fit and no method could separate them, and where none fits.

Play becomes less rule-governed as the payoffs grow (Fig.~\ref{fig:strategy}a,
Table~\ref{tab:strategy}). Pooling models, the share matched exactly by one
canonical rule falls from $31.4\%$ at $\lam = 0.01$ to $19.5\%$ at
$\lam = 10^{3}$, and the share matched by none rises from $54.4\%$ to $69.2\%$.
Per model the trend is significant and negative in Claude Haiku 4.5
($\beta = -0.545$ per decade of $\lam$), Qwen3 235B-A22B ($-0.267$) and Gemini
3.5 Flash-Lite ($-0.242$). It is significant and positive in Gemini 3.1
Flash-Lite ($+0.125$), and absent in GPT-5.4 Nano ($p = 0.44$). The heterogeneity is the
same one seen in the cooperation rates, and it would again cancel under
pooling.

The continuous version of the same measurement needs no labels at all: the
number of rounds that violate the nearest canonical rule
(Fig.~\ref{fig:strategy}b). It rises from 0.48 to 1.37 in Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite
and from 0.27 to 1.14 in Qwen3 235B-A22B between the smallest and largest
scale, with significant positive trends there and in Claude Haiku 4.5. Because
this quantity is computed by exact rule matching, it does not depend on the
classifier at all, which is why we lead the strategy analysis with it.

The composition shifts too, and in a consistent direction: away from
unconditional and towards reciprocal play (Fig.~\ref{fig:mix}). Pooled
across models, AllC falls from $45.6\%$ to $40.4\%$ of agent-games and
Tit-for-Tat rises from $9.2\%$ to $13.0\%$ between the extreme scales. The
per-model movements are larger and again not uniform. GPT-5.4 Nano moves from
$29.8\%$ to $50.8\%$ AllC while its AllD share falls from $46.0\%$ to $17.5\%$.
Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite moves the other way on AllC, from $73.8\%$ to $51.5\%$,
with Tit-for-Tat roughly doubling from $6.8\%$ to $14.3\%$.

This last result is the one in the paper that leans hardest on the learned
component, and the provenance is not uniform across the four labels. Of the
agent-games carrying the AllC label, $26.0\%$ were deduced from an exact rule
match and a further $29.6\%$ came from a rule set the LSTM ranked within; for
AllD the deduced share is $32.7\%$. For Tit-for-Tat and Win-Stay-Lose-Shift,
however, $95.4\%$ and $97.3\%$ of the labels are attributions the LSTM makes on
trajectories that match no canonical rule at all. A movement in the
Tit-for-Tat share is therefore a statement about which canonical rule the play
most resembles, not a claim that the model is running Tit-for-Tat, and we do
not read it as one.

\subsection{The effect is present on the opening move}

A rescaling could act through the course of play, by changing how a model reads
its opponent's history, or it could act on the reading of the matrix itself.
The opening move separates the two, because at round 1 no history exists and
nothing but the matrix and the instructions has been seen.

The effect is there already (Fig.~\ref{fig:firstmove}). In four models of five the range of opening-move cooperation across the ten
scales exceeds the range over rounds 2 to 10. The factor is 3.7 in Gemini 3.1
Flash-Lite, 2.0 in Claude Haiku 4.5, 1.9 in Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and 1.3 in
GPT-5.4 Nano. The
exception is Qwen3 235B-A22B, whose opening move is almost entirely determined
by its persona and therefore sits at 0.50 by construction at nearly every
scale, leaving little room to vary.

The reading we take from this is deflationary about dynamics. Whatever the payoff scale does, it does most of it before the game has begun.
That timing is consistent with the models forming an initial disposition by
reading the magnitudes off the matrix, and inconsistent with an account in
which the scale changes how history is weighed.

\section{Discussion}

The measurement reported here is unusual in having a null that is a theorem.
That property is what lets a movement of 0.243 in cooperation be read as a
departure from invariance rather than as the difference between two conditions. All five
models depart, and none is close to invariant. The departures are structured:
they enter at the opening move, they scale with the prompt language, they
reverse the persona instruction below a magnitude boundary, and they change
which canonical strategy the transcript matches.

The practical consequence is for how such numbers are reported. A cooperation
rate published for a language model is currently understood as a property of
the model. On this evidence it is a property of the model, the units the matrix
was written in, and the language it was posed in, jointly. Two laboratories
running the same protocol with matrices differing by a constant factor would
report cooperation rates differing by up to 0.24 and would have no way to tell,
from the numbers alone, that they had not disagreed about the model. We would
suggest that a scale-sensitivity measurement accompany any published
cooperation rate, in the same way an error bar does, and that it be measured
per model rather than pooled.

The pooling result deserves emphasis because it is the mechanism by which this
effect could have gone unnoticed. The scale has no significant main effect in
our data, and a study designed to detect a main effect would have correctly
found none while every model in it was moving substantially. Heterogeneous
effects that cancel are invisible to pooled designs by construction, and the
remedy is not a larger sample but a per-model analysis.

Two observations point beyond benchmark hygiene. The first is that the boundary
gating the persona instruction is one of magnitude, not of notation. Sub-unit
payoffs behave differently from supra-unit ones, and $\lam = 0.25$, still
printed with a decimal point, already behaves like the large scales. The second is that larger payoffs make play measurably less like any
canonical memory-one rule while shifting the mix towards reciprocity. Both are
consistent with the models treating the printed magnitudes as carrying
information about how much is at stake, which is precisely the information a
positive rescaling does not contain.

The limitations are those of the design. Five models cannot support claims
about families or about parameter count, and the specific shapes we report are
properties of these five. One game, one prompt template family and one horizon
of ten rounds leave the generality of the shapes untested, though the
consistency of the qualitative findings across five models and five languages
argues they are not artefacts of a single configuration. We measure departures
from an invariance; we do not identify the mechanism that produces them, and
nothing here distinguishes a tokenisation effect on numerals from a learned
association between large stakes and caution. Finally, our reference for
"large" is the sampling variability of these data, through the dyad bootstrap
and the permutation null, rather than an independent repeat of the whole
protocol. A measured test-retest floor from re-run sweeps would be a stronger
comparison, and it is the obvious next measurement.

The strategy read-out carries its own caveat, which is why its provenance is
reported. Only $23.0\%$ of agent-games are matched exactly by a single
canonical rule; $13.2\%$ are matched by several at once, which no method can
separate, and $63.7\%$ by none. The claims we draw from it are deliberately the
ones that survive those numbers. The share of exact matches and the distance to
the nearest rule are computed without any learned component; the compositional
claim is the weakest of the three and is presented as such. The asymmetry
between labels sharpens that caveat rather than softening it: the AllC and AllD
labels carry deduced shares of $26.0\%$ and $32.7\%$, while Tit-for-Tat and
Win-Stay-Lose-Shift are $95.4\%$ and $97.3\%$ attributions to trajectories no
canonical rule fits. Any reading of the compositional result as evidence that
these models implement Tit-for-Tat would be unsupported by our data.

\section{Materials and methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\paragraph{Corpus.} Games follow the FAIRGAME
protocol~\citep{fairgame2025} for the iterated prisoner's dilemma without
communication, with the number of rounds announced. The design crosses ten
payoff scales $\lam \in \{0.01, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 100, 1000\}$, five
prompt languages, four persona pairings and ten replicates, at ten rounds per
game, for the five models named in the figures. Each of the 250
model-by-language-by-scale cells contains exactly 200 dyads. The ingest refuses
to write its tables if any cell departs from that, if any game is not ten
rounds long, or if the payoff scale recovered from the recorded payoffs
disagrees with the one the cell was run at. Decisions are recorded as OptionA
or OptionB and mapped to defection and cooperation respectively, following the
penalty framing of the prompt.

\paragraph{Uncertainty.} The two agents of a dyad play each other, and the ten
rounds within an agent-game are autocorrelated. All intervals therefore
resample whole dyads with replacement (1{,}500 draws, percentile
method~\citep{efron1993introduction}), and all regressions cluster standard
errors on the dyad. The permutation test
shuffles the payoff-scale label within language and persona, which leaves the
design intact and destroys only the association under test, and uses 2{,}000
draws. The variance decomposition is a type-II analysis of variance on the 250
cell means, where the design is exactly balanced.

\paragraph{Strategy read-out.} Each agent-game is encoded as a sequence of
tokens pairing the previous round's joint outcome with the current action, an
alphabet shared between real transcripts and a synthetic corpus. A rule base
then asks, for each of AllC, AllD, Tit-for-Tat and Win-Stay-Lose-Shift, whether
the trajectory is exactly what that rule would have played given the history
that actually occurred. The answer is a set rather than a label, because over
ten rounds several canonical rules routinely prescribe the same moves. A player
who cooperated throughout against a cooperator is exactly consistent with AllC,
Tit-for-Tat and Win-Stay-Lose-Shift at once, and reporting all three is correct
rather than a failure. A single-layer LSTM~\citep{hochreiter1997long}
(24-dimensional embeddings, 96 hidden units) ranks within that set, and decides
alone only when no rule fits. It is trained on synthetic trajectories of the
four rules at zero and 5\% execution noise, balanced at 40{,}320 sequences per
strategy per level. It reaches $98.5\%$ accuracy on a held-out split, $93.6\%$
at an unseen 10\% noise level and $81.1\%$ at 20\%. No language-model
transcript is used in training.
