Summary
The paper investigates whether frontier language-model agents respect payoff-scale invariance in the iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma, a transformation that theoretically should have exactly zero behavioral effect (multiplying all payoffs by a positive constant). Using a large, balanced factorial design across five models, ten payoff scales spanning five orders of magnitude, five languages, and simple persona prompts, the authors find sizable, model-specific changes in cooperation, strong cross-lingual differences in sensitivity, and a pronounced regime shift when all printed payoffs are sub-unit (< 1). They further show that changes appear already at the opening move, that strategy resemblance (to memory-one rules) shifts with scale, and that cooperation appears driven more by self-persistence than by reciprocity; an evolutionary baseline moves in the opposite direction to the empirical findings.

Strengths
Technical novelty and innovation

Turning a game-theoretic invariance (positive scaling of payoffs) into a falsifiable, zero-effect experimental manipulation is an elegant and original methodological contribution.
The decomposition of conditional cooperation into reciprocity vs. persistence vs. matching is a clear, interpretable lens on mechanisms, sharpening the claim about “cooperation without reciprocity.”
The per-model analysis that documents heterogeneity and the pitfalls of pooling is carefully executed and conceptually important.
Experimental rigor and validation

Large-scale, exactly balanced grid: 10,000 dyads for the five main models (200,000 decisions) crossed by scale, language, and persona, with careful resampling (whole-dyad bootstrap) and clustered inference.
Clear statistical tests (permutation tests under an invariance null; Wald tests for categorical contrasts) and thoughtful variance decomposition to identify where effects live (model vs. language vs. scale interactions).
Strategy read-out with multiple provenances (exact matches vs. ambiguous vs. unmatched + LSTM arbitration) and continuous measures (violations) provides nuanced interpretation rather than over-claiming identification.
Clarity of presentation

Careful distinction between what is deduced exactly vs. what is attributed by a learned classifier; explicit caveats about provenance of labels.
Transparent acknowledgment of prompt-template artifacts and model-endpoint caveats; clean figures that foreground heterogeneity and regime shifts.
Significance of contributions

Demonstrates that a theoretically inert manipulation meaningfully changes LLM-agents’ behavior; the immediate policy implication is that reported cooperation rates for agentic LLMs can be scale-sensitive, hence measurement and deployment choices matter.
Cross-lingual sensitivity and persona-gating by scale challenge assumptions of robustness and raise governance concerns for multilingual, agentic deployments.
Weaknesses
Technical limitations or concerns

The strongest claim “cooperate without reciprocity” risks overstatement: reciprocity is reduced but not absent for some models; results would be stronger with effect-size CIs and direct tests per model supporting “near-zero” reciprocity where claimed.
The identified scale boundary coincides with decimal, sub-unit numerals; potential confounds (numeric formatting, decimal salience, unit framing) are not fully controlled (e.g., integer-only presentations via unit conversion).
The evolutionary baseline restricts the strategy set to four canonical rules; given the empirical finding that agents condition predominantly on their own past action, enriching the baseline with persistence-biased or non-reactive strategies might change the comparison.
Experimental gaps or methodological issues

Model endpoints are served via a proxy with non-pinned identifiers; one model is relegated to appendix due to a “provisional” label. This undermines replicability and stability of claims tied to specific models.
Temperature is fixed at 1.0 for all models; no robustness checks on temperature or decoding settings are provided, though these plausibly interact with numerical salience and persona framing.
The fallback-to-Option-A parsing policy can depress measured cooperation and is not logged at the cell level; without per-cell fallback rates it is hard to fully exclude correlations with small scales or specific languages.
Clarity or presentation issues

While generally excellent, a few statements could be tempered (e.g., “cooperate without reciprocity”) to better match the quantitative evidence showing smaller—but not absent—partner effects for some models.
The short justification that agents “experience the true dilemma” via Spearman correlations is terse; richer comprehension diagnostics (as used in some related work) would reassure readers.
Missing related work or comparisons

Prior FAIRGAME scaling results (λ in {0.1, 1, 10}) observed reduced cooperation at low stakes; this paper substantially generalizes and reframes, but an explicit, quantitative bridge to those findings would help.
Recent evidence on phrasing/anchoring in IPD prompts (e.g., neutral vs. canonical labels) bears directly on the round-1 effects; explicit ablations using neutral, unit-normalized presentations could disentangle scaling from semantic anchoring.
Detailed Comments
Technical soundness evaluation

The central test—scaling by λ and expecting zero effect—is sound and theoretically well-motivated. The empirical rejections are statistically robust within this dataset.
The decomposition of conditional cooperation into partner and own main effects is technically clean and interpretable; the caution about p_CD − p_DC not being a reciprocity measure is a valuable corrective.
The strategy read-out pipeline is responsibly presented. Still, since many TFT/WSLS attributions occur in unmatched trajectories, the authors rightly frame these as resemblances. The continuous “rounds violating nearest rule” metric usefully reduces dependence on any specific classifier.
Experimental evaluation assessment

The design is impressively comprehensive and balanced, with appropriate clustered inference and permutation tests that match the invariance null.
Two notable potential confounds warrant further checks: (i) numerical formatting and sub-unit decimals (e.g., 0.6, 0.2) vs. integers; (ii) the cell-level rate of parsing fallbacks, which default to defection and could systematically vary with scale or language. Recording and reporting fallback rates per cell would strengthen the conclusions.
Robustness to decoding parameters (temperature/top-p) is untested; given known sensitivity of LLM behavior to these levers, at least a small-scale robustness slice would be informative.
Comparison with related work (using the summaries provided)

FAIRGAME (2504.14325) and its extension (2512.07462) reported payoff sensitivity (especially reduced cooperation at λ = 0.1) and cross-lingual effects; the present paper substantially scales up the grid (10 scales × 5 languages), reframes scaling as a theorem-backed null, and emphasizes per-model heterogeneity and the dangers of pooling.
Fontana et al. (2406.13605) study 100-round IPD with opponent-parameterized stochastic agents and meta-prompting for state understanding. Here, the emphasis is on a logically inert manipulation and on round-1 anchoring, which complements their longer-horizon behavioral profiling.
Work on anchoring via canonical labels (2605.00436) finds that explicit “IPD” phrasing can override provided payoffs; the present paper’s finding that scale effects manifest on round 1 is consistent with pre-play priors and salience. An ablation adopting their neutral phrasing would triangulate whether the observed scale effects are primarily numerical salience or semantic anchoring.
Personality steering (2601.05302) demonstrates that simple persona prompts modulate cooperation; this paper crucially shows that the effectiveness and even the sign of persona steering can flip with scale, adding an important layer to persona-based control.
ALIGN (2602.07777) and LLMsPark (2509.16610) focus on broader multi-agent governance and game suites; the present contribution is more surgical—isolating a theoretically null manipulation in a canonical game—but the governance implications (reported cooperation is scale-sensitive and language-dependent) are highly complementary.
Discussion of broader impact and significance

The invariance-violation result challenges the robustness of LLM-agent behavioral audits: reported cooperation rates are not portable across units/scales, languages, or even model updates, which directly impacts evaluation, deployment, and governance.
The persona-gating by numeric magnitude suggests that simple instruction-based control of agent dispositions is unreliable under seemingly innocuous formatting changes.
The mechanism analysis (persistence > reciprocity) indicates that observed cooperation in these agents is not primarily reciprocal; this matters for designing institutions and mechanisms that assume tit-for-tat-like behavior.
Questions for Authors
Can you report, per model×language×scale cell, the rate of parsing fallbacks and regeneration attempts? This would help rule out correlations between higher fallback rates (which default to defection) and the sub-unit regime.
Could you add an ablation that preserves integer formatting across scales via unit conversion (e.g., “months” vs. “years”) or uses spelled-out numbers, to disentangle numerical formatting (decimals, leading zeros) from true magnitude effects?
How stable are the results to decoding parameters (temperature/top-p)? Do the scale effects persist, attenuate, or amplify at lower temperatures?
For the reciprocity/persistence analysis, can you provide confidence intervals and significance tests per model for each contrast, and clarify which models meaningfully differ from zero reciprocity?
The Arabic/Chinese templates invert the goal statement (maximize reward vs minimize penalty). Can you supply a harmonized template ablation to show that language-by-scale sensitivity patterns are not driven by this inconsistency?
The evolutionary baseline excludes persistence-biased strategies. Would incorporating such strategies (e.g., “stick-with-own-last”) materially change the baseline trend with λ and the claimed “opposite direction” result?
Since the scale effect is pronounced on move 1, did you try pre-play comprehension checks (e.g., questions about smallest vs. largest penalties, which action minimizes expected penalty) as in recent work, to ascertain whether models truly parse the matrix at each scale?
Given the provisional nature of some endpoints, can you provide a replication on public, pinned model versions (even if fewer models) to strengthen reproducibility of the main claims?
Overall Assessment
This paper offers a compelling, conceptually novel and empirically well-executed study: it operationalizes a theoretical zero-effect manipulation (payoff scaling) and shows that contemporary LLM agents violate payoff-scale invariance in sizeable, heterogeneous, and linguistically sensitive ways, with changes already evident on move 1 and with persona-contingent sign reversals at sub-unit scales. The analysis is careful about heterogeneity and pooling, uses appropriate inference, and contributes a transparent mechanism lens (reciprocity vs. persistence) alongside a principled evolutionary baseline that moves in the opposite direction. The main reservations concern potential confounds (numeric formatting, fallback parsing rates), endpoint reproducibility, and somewhat strong phrasing about “without reciprocity.” These are addressable and, in my opinion, do not blunt the central findings. Overall, the work is timely, impactful for the ACL community’s growing interest in agentic LLM behavior, and sets a high bar for methodological rigor in behavioral audits. I recommend acceptance, with requested clarifications and a few targeted ablations to consolidate the claims.
