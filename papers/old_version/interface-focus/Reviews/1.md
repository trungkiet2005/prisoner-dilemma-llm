Summary
This paper investigates whether large language model (LLM) agents respect the payoff-scale invariance of von Neumann–Morgenstern utility in the iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma (IPD). Using a balanced, large-scale factorial design (6 models, 10 payoff scales spanning five orders of magnitude, 5 prompt languages, 4 persona pairings; 12,000 dyads and 240,000 decisions), the authors show that all evaluated models substantially violate scale invariance, with heterogeneous, model-specific and language-dependent responses. They further find that the effect is already present on the opening move, gates the effect of persona prompts (including sign reversals around a sub-unit boundary), and alters the resemblance of play to canonical memory-one strategies; an evolutionary baseline predicts the opposite direction, highlighting that LLM cooperation appears to be driven by self-persistence rather than reciprocity.

Strengths
Technical novelty and innovation
Introduces payoff scaling as a principled “zero-effect” manipulation based on a normative invariance theorem, turning a classical axiom into an experimental instrument for auditing LLM agents.
Moves beyond aggregate cooperation rates to strategy-level read-outs (rule-matching + LSTM arbitration), plus a carefully analyzed decomposition of conditional cooperation into reciprocity/persistence/matching.
Provides an analytical evolutionary-game-theory baseline solved on the same grid to contextualize observed behavior.
Experimental rigor and validation
Large, balanced factorial design across models, scales, languages, and personas with substantial sample size (240k decisions) and completeness checks.
Sound statistical choices: whole-dyad bootstrapping, permutation tests keyed to the invariance null, clustered standard errors, and variance decomposition.
Common-random-numbers scheme and explicit reporting of parsing fallback logic; completeness guard verifies correct scaling per cell.
Clarity of presentation
Clear articulation of the invariance construction and why it yields a sharp, quantitative null.
Careful provenance of strategy labels; explicit caveats about what the classifier can and cannot infer; continuous distance-to-rule metrics complement discrete labels.
Transparent acknowledgement of prompt-language template artifacts and endpoint caveats for particular models.
Significance of contributions
Establishes robust evidence that LLM strategic behavior is sensitive to absolute payoff magnitude, contradicting a theoretically invariant manipulation.
Identifies that language and personas interact nontrivially with scale, implying deployment-relevant biases for multilingual and agentic use-cases.
The “cooperate without reciprocity” finding (self-persistence dominates partner-responsiveness) is an important mechanistic insight for safety/governance and multi-agent modeling.
Weaknesses
Technical limitations or concerns
Model identity/reproducibility concerns: endpoints accessed via a proxy with provisional/undated identifiers (e.g., “Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite,” “GPT-5.4 Nano,” “Grok 4.20”), making strict replication difficult and potentially undermining claims about “frontier” models.
The PD is framed as penalties and includes language-template inconsistencies (Arabic/Chinese goal phrasing), potentially conflating language effects with framing mismatches; although analyses are within-language, this still complicates interpretation.
The persona prompt semantics appear ambiguous in many settings (e.g., who to be “selfish” toward), and some models invert persona effects; without stronger manipulation checks, the persona-gating result may reflect uncontrolled semantic variation.
Experimental gaps or methodological issues
Decoding settings fixed (temperature = 1.0) without ablation; no analysis of sensitivity to sampling parameters that are known to modulate LLM behavior.
Only self-play (same-model dyads) is considered; no cross-model interactions to test generalization of scale sensitivity against heterogeneous partners.
Ten-round known horizon only; horizon effects can alter end-game incentives and reciprocity; although the opening-move analysis is strong, longer/unknown horizons could clarify dynamic impacts.
Strategy attribution is limited to four memory-one canonicals; emergent or ZD/extortionate strategies, mixed policies, and memory > 1 may be underdetected, even though a continuous violation metric partly mitigates this.
Clarity or presentation issues
Some figures and model names are unconventional; occasional heavy narrative style can obscure precise experimental details (e.g., exact fallback rates not logged per cell).
The strong phrasing “cooperate without reciprocity” may be overstated: reciprocity is often positive but weaker than self-persistence; the paper later nuances this but headlines could be tempered.
Missing related work or comparisons
Prior FAIRGAME-based studies have already probed scale sensitivity (albeit at fewer scales) and multilingual effects; while the present work substantially extends breadth and introduces the invariance framing, a more explicit positioning against these works’ key conclusions would help.
Connections to models of numeracy and magnitude perception in LLMs (e.g., numeric salience, formatting-induced reasoning shifts) could be deepened as potential mechanisms for sub-unit boundary effects.
Detailed Comments
Technical soundness evaluation
The invariance test is principled and compelling: scaling by λ > 0 should be behaviorally irrelevant in VNM terms, so deviations are diagnostic. The use of a categorical λ factor alongside log-scale trends is appropriate given the observed nonlinearity (sub-unit boundary).
The statistical treatment respects dependencies (dyad bootstraps, clustered errors), and permutation tests tailored to the invariance null are apt. The ANOVA variance partition usefully shows that model-by-scale interactions dominate main effects, cautioning against pooled reporting.
Strategy inference is conservative: provenance tracking, rule-matching before learning, and a continuous mismatch metric improve robustness. Still, restriction to four canonicals is a limitation; the paper appropriately refrains from overinterpreting TFT/WSLS labels.
Experimental evaluation assessment
The dataset is large and balanced, with careful completeness checks. Evidence for violation of scale invariance is strong across models (including opening move), languages, and personas, with substantial effect sizes (up to 0.636 swing).
The language-template inconsistency (penalties vs reward phrasing) is rightly treated as part of the stimulus; within-language scale effects mitigate this confound for the main claims. Still, a corrective control (uniformly fixed goal phrasing across languages) would strengthen causal attribution.
The persona-gating phenomenon and especially the sign flips at sub-unit scales are striking; however, stronger manipulation checks (e.g., post-hoc content analysis of justifications, probing “who” the agent is selfish/cooperative toward) would bolster this result against semantic ambiguity concerns.
Comparison with related work (using the summaries provided)
Closely related studies (e.g., FAIRGAME extensions, payoff-scaled IPD with λ ∈ {0.1,1,10}) reported stake sensitivity and language effects across fewer scales and models; this paper provides a substantially larger grid, a stronger invariance framing, and a rich strategy- and mechanism-level analysis (opening move, reciprocity vs persistence), representing a meaningful advance.
Multilingual robustness studies (e.g., cross-lingual stability, calibration invariance) similarly find language-dependent behavior; the present work complements these by isolating a theoretically “null” manipulation and showing language-dependent violations, suggesting deeper language–numeracy interactions.
Evolutionary and active-inference baselines (e.g., finite-population dynamics, AIF dependence on payoff precision β) tend to predict that higher effective selection/β favors defection or delays cooperation. The paper’s empirical opposite trend, coupled with weak reciprocity, is a notable mechanistic divergence relevant to agent design and governance.
Discussion of broader impact and significance
The findings have practical implications for deploying LLM agents in negotiation/coordination: “stakes” formatting and numeric magnitude become consequential levers with no grounding in rational-choice theory, potentially leading to brittleness and unforeseen biases across jurisdictions/languages.
The result that models are more self-persistent than reciprocating suggests that cooperation may persist for reasons orthogonal to equilibrium reciprocity, which could both produce unexpected prosociality and vulnerability to exploitation, depending on context.
The paper’s demonstration that pooled averages can mislead (reporting a curve no model follows) is an important methodological lesson for evaluation papers and benchmark design.
Questions for Authors
Can you provide stable, versioned identifiers (and, if possible, hashes) for all model endpoints to improve reproducibility, or alternatively re-run a subset on fully versioned APIs to verify that the key findings persist?
How sensitive are your results to decoding parameters (e.g., temperature, top-p)? Have you tested at least a low-temperature setting to examine whether scale invariance violations shrink under more deterministic decoding?
Could you run a language-harmonization control where the Arabic/Chinese prompts’ goal phrasing is corrected (penalty minimization consistently stated) to quantify how much of the language effect remains?
To disentangle magnitude from decimal notation, can you add conditions using integers below 1 (e.g., “months,” “days,” or spelled-out units) or scientific notation, and a textual “units are arbitrary; only relative differences matter” disclaimer, to test the numeracy/salience hypothesis?
For persona effects, can you include comprehension checks or post-hoc content analyses to verify whether “selfish/cooperative” was interpreted consistently (e.g., toward the partner vs authorities) and whether interpretation changes across λ?
Your reciprocity analysis shows persistence > reciprocity; can you report per-round evolution of these contrasts and whether they vary materially with horizon (e.g., T=10 vs T=50) to separate initial stance from dynamic updating?
How robust are strategy resemblance shifts if you expand the strategy set (e.g., extortionate/ZD, Grim Trigger, Generous TFT) or use a multi-label classifier that can represent mixtures?
Does cross-model play (heterogeneous dyads) qualitatively change the scale effect (e.g., when a more cooperative model meets a more hawkish one) or the reciprocity vs persistence balance?
Do you observe similar scale-sensitivity patterns in other games with invariant positive scaling (e.g., Public Goods or Battle of the Sexes), suggesting a general numeracy/framing mechanism?
Can you disclose the per-cell parsing fallback rates to rule out that subtle variation in unparseable outputs correlates with λ and biases estimates?
Overall Assessment

(Calibrated to ICLR scale)
This is a careful, ambitious, and timely empirical study that leverages a theoretically sharp invariance to reveal consequential, heterogeneous, and language-dependent violations in LLM agent behavior. The design is large-scale and balanced; analyses are thoughtful, with appropriate statistics and interpretability tools. The opening-move sensitivity, persona sign-flips around a sub-unit boundary, and the reciprocity-vs-persistence decomposition are especially compelling. The main reservations concern reproducibility and construct validity: some model endpoints are not stably identifiable; persona semantics and language-template inconsistencies complicate interpretation; and decoding-parameter ablations are absent. Nonetheless, the core finding—that LLM agents do not respect payoff-scale invariance and that this interacts with language and persona—is strongly supported and important for the ICLR community working on LLM agents, evaluation, and governance. I recommend acceptance, preferably with revisions addressing reproducibility, decoding ablations, and additional controls for language/persona semantics.
