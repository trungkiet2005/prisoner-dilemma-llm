\section{Supplement to the methods}\label{appendix:methodology}

\subsection{Scope of the corpus and the completeness guard}\label{appendix:corpus}

The corpus is a complete factorial crossing of six language models, ten payoff
scales, five prompt languages, four ordered persona pairings and ten
replicates. The unit of collection is the dyad, one ten-round game between two
agents of the same model; it contributes two agent-games, an agent-game being
the ten decisions one agent takes in one game. Each of the 300
model-by-language-by-scale cells holds exactly 40 dyads, so the corpus is
12{,}000 dyads, 24{,}000 agent-games and 240{,}000 decisions
(table~\ref{tab:S-corpus}). The five models the main text reports carry 250 of
those cells, 10{,}000 dyads, 20{,}000 agent-games and 200{,}000 decisions.

Balance is enforced rather than assumed. The analysis build refuses to write
any table unless two conditions hold. First, every model-by-language-by-scale
cell contains exactly 40 dyads, with none missing and none duplicated. Second,
the payoffs recorded in each game recover, to a relative tolerance of $10^{-6}$,
the payoff scale that cell was run at, which catches a mislabelled or
misfiled run rather than trusting the directory name. The guard exists because
the corpus was collected in batches over several sessions and an incomplete
batch is otherwise invisible in an aggregate.

The polarity of the action mapping is load-bearing for every cooperation rate
in this article, so it was checked twice and in two ways. In the prompt,
Option~A pays less in both columns of a penalty matrix that the agent is told
to minimise, and is therefore the dominant, defecting action, while mutual
Option~B at $2\lam$ beats mutual Option~A at $6\lam$ and is the cooperative
one. In the data, the realised penalties recorded for each pair of actions
reproduce that matrix. Cooperation means Option~B throughout.

The empirical logs confirm that the models experience the actual strategic
tension of the prisoner's dilemma rather than an artefact of prompt confusion.
Own cooperation correlates with own utility at Spearman $\rho = -0.015$,
confirming that unilateral cooperation is privately unrewarding. Conversely, the
opponent's cooperation correlates with own utility at Spearman $\rho = +0.916$,
confirming that the partner's move governs individual payoff. At the dyad level,
total welfare correlates with joint cooperation at Spearman $\rho = +0.985$,
demonstrating that mutual cooperation maximises collective surplus. The empirical
incentive structure thus faithfully reproduces the theoretical dilemma.

The repository records corpus assembly between 2 August and 5 September 2026.
Those commit dates bound the archived collection work, but the raw files do not
retain per-request timestamps. Exact provider request dates therefore cannot be
reconstructed from the release.

The release records matching by game identifier across payoff scales. It does
not retain the API seed values, so it supports a matched-block analysis but
does not verify provider-specific seed semantics. The robustness analysis
therefore permutes payoff-scale labels within recorded game-ID blocks.

With 10,000 blocked permutations, the scale range remains significant for all
six models: $p=0.0023$ for Claude Haiku 4.5 and $p=0.0001$ for each of the
other five models, the permutation floor. The ranges are unchanged because the
analysis only conditions the null on the recorded matching structure.

Decisions are elicited as free text and parsed by the protocol's own matcher. A
reply the matcher cannot resolve is regenerated, up to two further attempts,
and if all three attempts fail the decision is recorded as Option~A, which
under this framing is defection. The direction of the resulting bias is
therefore known: the fallback can only push a measured cooperation rate down,
never up, and it cannot manufacture the cooperative behaviour reported in the
main text. Each collection run checks its own fallback rate against a tolerance
of 0.02 once the sweep has finished, and records a failed assertion in its
benchmark report if the rate exceeds it. That check flags a run rather than
halting it, and the achieved rates are not carried in the released per-game
tables, so we cannot report how they vary with the payoff scale. If the fallback rate were itself to rise with the printed magnitude of
the payoffs, it would bias the smallest measured ranges downward rather than
create them.

\subsection{Prompt templates and a framing artefact}\label{appendix:templates}

The prompt templates are byte-identical to the resource files of the FAIRGAME
protocol~\cite{buscemi2025fairgame} and were checked against them before every
collection run, by a test that compares the rendered template with the resource
file byte for byte. The check exists because the templates carry Arabic,
Chinese and Vietnamese text inline, and a single save in a non-UTF-8 encoding
silently corrupts the prompt actually sent to the model rather than only a
comment.

Artefacts of the published templates are reproduced rather than repaired, so
that the corpus matches the protocol as published. The consequential one is
that the Arabic and Chinese versions state the four cells as penalties while
phrasing the goal sentence as maximising a reward. We therefore treat the five
languages as five distinct stimuli rather than as five renderings of one, and
we do not attribute a difference in the \emph{level} of cooperation between two
languages to the language alone. The wording is fixed within each language
across the ten payoff scales, so it does not invalidate the within-language
rescaling comparison. It may nevertheless interact with numerical magnitude;
consequently, cross-language differences in scale sensitivity cannot be
attributed to language alone. Every scale-sensitivity quantity reported in the
main text is computed within a language.

The persona line is a single sentence in the language of the prompt, naming
the agent's own disposition and never the opponent's. In English the two forms
are \emph{You are cooperative.} and \emph{You are selfish.} Because the
opponent's persona is never disclosed, a persona pairing reaches a player only
through realised behaviour, and not before round 2.

\subsection{The rule base}\label{appendix:rule_based}

Each agent-game is encoded as a sequence of ten two-letter tokens of the form
$\langle$previous outcome$\rangle\langle$current action$\rangle$, over the
outcome letters $E$ (no history yet), $R$ (both cooperated), $S$ (the player
cooperated and was exploited), $T$ (the player exploited a cooperator) and $P$
(both defected), and the actions $C$ and $D$. The vocabulary is therefore
eleven symbols including padding, and it is shared exactly between the
synthetic training corpus and the language-model transcripts, which is what
makes a classifier trained on the former applicable to the latter.

All four canonical strategies are memory-one, so each prescribes an action as a
function of the previous outcome letter alone (table~\ref{tab:A-rules}).
Deciding whether a trajectory is \emph{exactly} what a strategy would have
played on the history that actually occurred is then a per-token lookup, with
no learning and no tolerance parameter.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\footnotesize
\caption{\textbf{The four canonical memory-one rules.} The action each rule
prescribes given the previous round's joint outcome. Tit-for-Tat and
Win-Stay-Lose-Shift differ only in the two columns $T$ and $P$, which is why
ten rounds of mutual cooperation are consistent with AllC, Tit-for-Tat and
Win-Stay-Lose-Shift at once.}
\label{tab:A-rules}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule
& $E$ (first move) & $R$ & $S$ & $T$ & $P$ \\
\midrule
AllC & $C$ & $C$ & $C$ & $C$ & $C$ \\
AllD & $D$ & $D$ & $D$ & $D$ & $D$ \\
Tit-for-Tat & $C$ & $C$ & $D$ & $C$ & $D$ \\
Win-Stay-Lose-Shift & $C$ & $C$ & $D$ & $D$ & $C$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Formally, writing $\mathbf{a} = (a_1, \ldots, a_N)$ for the agent's actions and
$\mathbf{o} = (o_1, \ldots, o_N)$ for the opponent's, and $\pi_k(\cdot)$ for the
action prescribed by rule $k$ in table~\ref{tab:A-rules}, the match is
\[
  \text{consistent}(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{o}, k)
  \;\equiv\;
  \bigwedge_{t=1}^{N} \bigl[\, a_t = \pi_k(e_t) \,\bigr],
  \qquad
  e_1 = E, \quad
  e_t = \mathrm{outcome}(a_{t-1}, o_{t-1}) \ \ (t \geq 2),
\]
and the deviation count that the main text calls the rule distance is the same
expression with the conjunction replaced by a sum of indicators,
$d_k = \sum_{t=1}^{N} \mathbb{I}[a_t \neq \pi_k(e_t)]$, reported as
$\min_k d_k$, the number of rounds violating the nearest canonical rule.

Because several rules routinely prescribe the same actions on the history that
occurred, the matcher returns a \emph{set} rather than a label. A player who
cooperated in every round against a cooperator is exactly consistent with AllC,
Tit-for-Tat and Win-Stay-Lose-Shift simultaneously, and no method, learned or
otherwise, can separate them from that evidence; reporting the set is the
correct answer rather than a failure of the method. The three provenances are
recorded separately and never merged:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{deduced}, where exactly one rule fits, so the label is a deduction;
\item \emph{ambiguous}, where several fit, so the rules fix a candidate set and
      the classifier only ranks inside it;
\item \emph{unmatched}, where no rule fits, so the label is an attribution the
      classifier makes alone.
\end{itemize}

There is no confidence threshold anywhere in this read-out, and no composite
label is emitted: the multi-label answer is the set itself, and where a single
label is needed for a table it is the classifier's ranking restricted to that
set. The classifier can therefore never overturn a deduction.

\subsection{Classifier training and validation}\label{appendix:classifier}

The learned branch is trained on a synthetic corpus that contains no
language-model output at all. Trajectories of the four canonical strategies are
generated against random opponents at four execution-noise levels, balanced at
40{,}320 sequences per strategy per level, giving 161{,}280 sequences at each
level. Training uses the zero-noise and 5\% levels pooled, 322{,}560 sequences
split 80/20 at random, so the held-out split has 64{,}512 sequences; the 10\%
and 20\% levels are never trained on and serve as out-of-distribution tests.

The network is a single-layer unidirectional LSTM~\cite{lstm}: an
embedding of the eleven-symbol vocabulary into 24 dimensions, a recurrent
layer of 96 hidden units, dropout 0.15 and a linear map to the four classes.
It is optimised with AdamW at learning rate $3\times10^{-3}$ and weight decay
$10^{-4}$ under a cosine schedule, for 12 epochs at batch size 512, with seed
1234. Accuracy is 0.985 on the held-out split, 0.936 at 10\% execution noise
and 0.811 at 20\%, with per-class recall on the held-out split of 0.985 for
AllC, 0.995 for AllD, 0.974 for Tit-for-Tat and 0.983 for Win-Stay-Lose-Shift
(figure~\ref{fig:classifier} and table~\ref{tab:S-classifier}). The two
conditional rules are the harder pair,
as expected: an execution error in either can be read as the signature of the
other.

The 20\% level bounds where the read-out is warranted, and
\S\ref{appendix:robust_readout} states where the corpus sits relative to it.

\subsection{Resampling and testing}\label{appendix:stats}

Three dependencies rule out treating agent-games as independent: the two agents
of a dyad play each other, the ten rounds inside an agent-game are strongly
autocorrelated, and the ten replicates of a cell share a payoff scale. Primary
inferential intervals therefore use a nonparametric bootstrap that resamples
\emph{whole dyads} with replacement, 1{,}500 draws, percentile
method~\cite{efron1993introduction}, with a fixed random stream so that draws
are paired across cells. Descriptive figure intervals use whole-dyad resampling.
Primary per-model and trend regressions cluster standard errors on the dyad.
The supplementary scale-by-language robustness model instead clusters on the
documented language-by-game-ID blocks to preserve the matched ten-scale ladders.
As a robustness check, we also condition the
randomisation on the documented game-ID blocks, preserving each complete
ten-scale ladder. This sensitivity does not establish common-random-number
semantics, which are not retained in the released tables.

The range of a model's cooperation over the ten scales is a maximum minus a
minimum and so is positive by construction. Its bootstrap interval quantifies
the precision of the range, not its distance from zero, and the test is
supplied separately by a permutation. Because the null is a theorem rather than
a nuisance hypothesis, the permutation destroys exactly the association under
test and leaves the rest of the design as observed: the payoff-scale label is
shuffled among whole dyads within language-by-pairing strata, the unit to which
the scale was assigned, and the range of the ten scale means is recomputed.
With 2{,}000 draws the smallest attainable $p$ value is $1/2001 = 0.0005$.
Shuffling agent-games independently would be anti-conservative, because the two
agent-games of a dyad would then be free to land in different scale groups
although they were generated together.

The variance decomposition is a type-II analysis of variance on cell means with
terms for model, language and payoff scale and their three two-way
interactions, and no three-way term, so the residual is the omitted three-way
interaction: 144 degrees of freedom on the 250 cells of the five main-text
models, and 180 on all 300 cells.

\subsection{The strategy read-out, in full}\label{appendix:readout_full}

Table~\ref{tab:S-deduced} gives the share of agent-games matched exactly by one
canonical rule, by model and payoff scale, computed by exact matching with no

Table~\ref{tab:S-provenance} is the one that bounds how the composition may be

depend on the classifier at all.

\subsection{Why the model curves cancel under pooling}\label{appendix:pooling}

Tables~\ref{tab:S-shapes} and~\ref{tab:S-shapes-all} give the pairwise
correlation between the ten-point cooperation-against-scale curves of each pair
of models, for the five main-text models and for all six.

The cancellation is arithmetic. Writing $c_m(\lam)$ for model $m$'s curve and
$\bar{c}(\lam)$ for their average, the range of $\bar{c}$ is bounded above by
the average of the individual ranges, with equality only if the curves attain
their extremes at the same scales and move together in between. Here six of the
ten pairs are anticorrelated and the mean correlation is $-0.10$, so the bound
is far from tight: the pooled range is 0.154 against a mean within-model range
of 0.249, an attenuation of 1.62. With the sixth model included the same
quantities are 0.125, 0.219 and 1.76, with nine of the fifteen pairs negative,
so the pooling argument is stronger over the whole corpus than over the five
models the main text reports.

This is not a power problem. Collecting more dyads tightens the interval around
$\bar{c}$; it does not make $\bar{c}$ a better description of any $c_m$.

The cancellation of model curves under pooling reflects an underlying
structural divide in how models play: per-game cooperation is not a graded
Gaussian quantity, but a mixture over distinct policy regimes
(figure~\ref{fig:coop_dist}). Across all 24{,}000 agent-games, $11.1\%$ never
cooperate and $28.5\%$ always cooperate, so that two discrete boundary atoms
carry $39.5\%$ of all play against $18.2\%$ expected under a flat distribution
across the 11 attainable rates.

Crucially, this boundary concentration is a model-level trait rather than an
invariant property of the task. In Claude Haiku 4.5, boundary mass accounts for
only $8.6\%$ of play; its distribution is unimodal with an interior mode at
$0.2$, reflecting an adaptive policy that explores intermediate action
frequencies. In stark contrast, Qwen3 235B-A22B ($60.4\%$ boundary mass) and
Grok 4.20 ($59.8\%$ boundary mass) play predominantly degenerate pure
strategies, switching between always cooperating and always defecting. A single
pooled mean cooperation rate therefore conflates two distinct phenomena: how
often a model commits to an unconditional pure policy, and which pure policy it
selects.

\subsection{Outcome fairness, net exploitation and the V-shaped welfare frontier}\label{appendix:fairness_welfare}

Studies evaluating machine behaviour in multi-agent environments often report
outcome fairness, measured via the Gini coefficient or payoff disparity between
paired agents, interpreting lower disparity as evidence of egalitarian or
prosocial alignment. In symmetric normal-form games played between identical
agents, however, within-dyad inequality is not an independent behavioural
dimension: it is an exact algebraic function of one-directional exploitation.

Because both agents face identical payoff matrices, whenever both choose the
same action (mutual defection Option~A or mutual cooperation Option~B), their
per-round payoffs are identical by construction ($\pi_1 = \pi_2$). A payoff
disparity can only arise in asymmetric rounds where one agent cooperates while
the other defects. Across a ten-round repeated interaction, the relationship is
exact:
\begin{equation}
\text{signed\_gap} \;=\; p_{DC} - p_{CD}, \qquad
\text{utility\_gap} \;=\; |p_{DC} - p_{CD}| \;=\; p_{\text{exploit}} - 2 \min(p_{CD}, p_{DC}),
\label{eq:fairness_identity}
\end{equation}
where $p_{DC}$ and $p_{CD}$ denote the proportion of rounds in which the focal
agent exploits or is exploited by its partner, respectively. Across all
12{,}000 dyads in the corpus, the maximum residual from this identity is zero.
Over $72.8\%$ of dyads end with zero payoff gap, and the observed gap correlates
with total one-directional exploitation at $r = 0.941$. Within-dyad inequality
therefore measures coordination failure rather than a distributional preference.

Figure~\ref{fig:fairness_welfare} illustrates the resulting relationship between
fairness and joint welfare. Rather than a monotonic trade-off between equality
and efficiency, the empirical frontier traces a distinct V-shaped curve. In the
poorest welfare tercile, dyads are locked in mutual defection ($DD$), yielding
near-perfect equality ($\text{fairness} = 1 - \text{Gini} \approx 0.98$) at
minimal social surplus. As dyads begin to explore cooperation without mutual
coordination, unilateral exploitation spikes, causing fairness to reach its
minimum ($0.62$). Finally, as dyads achieve mutual cooperation ($CC$), joint
welfare peaks alongside a return to perfect equality ($\text{fairness} \approx 1.0$).

Crucially, this structural coordination mechanism explains why the payoff scale
exerts zero influence on within-dyad fairness: the regression slopes of both the
within-dyad utility gap and Gini coefficient against $\log_{10}\lam$ have slope
$0.0004$ ($p = 0.80$). We therefore detect no association between payoff scale
and this outcome. This empirical null is distinct from the algebraic identity
relating within-dyad inequality to unilateral exploitation.

\section{The evolutionary baseline}\label{appendix:egt}

The main text reports what six language models did. This appendix computes what
the same payoff matrices imply for a population of adaptive agents restricted
to the same four canonical rules, on the same ten payoff scales, and compares
the two. The purpose is to size a gap rather than to test either object:
the baseline says which strategy mixtures are evolutionarily stable when the
only sources of variation are payoff magnitude and stochastic execution, and
the difference between that and the corpus is a measurement of everything a
language model brings that an evolutionary model does not. Combining
evolutionary modelling with language-model simulation in this way has proved
informative in recent work on governance
dilemmas~\cite{balabanova2025media,buscemi2025llms}.

\subsection{The model}\label{appendix:egt_model}

We adopt the standard pairwise-comparison process under the small-mutation
limit~\cite{sigmund:2010bo,han_when_2021,nowak2004emergence,liu2020evolutionary},
in which a finite well-mixed population of size $Z = 100$ evolves through
social imitation governed by the Fermi update rule. The small-mutation limit is
the regime in which mutations are rare enough that the population spends almost
all of its time in monomorphic states, every individual playing the same
strategy, and only occasionally transitions between them; it has a clean
analytical solution and is the standard reference baseline in finite-population
evolutionary game theory, and it has proved able to reproduce features of human
experimental data~\cite{rand2013evolution,zisis2015generosity}. Selection
intensity is set to $\beta = 0.1$.

To put the analysis on the same incentive structure as the experiments, the
FAIRGAME penalty matrix is negated, so that lower-is-better penalties become
higher-is-better utilities. The penalties $T_{\text{pen}} = 0$,
$R_{\text{pen}} = 2$, $P_{\text{pen}} = 6$, $S_{\text{pen}} = 10$ map to
utilities $T = 0$, $R = -2$, $P = -6$, $S = -10$, which satisfy the standard
prisoner's dilemma ordering $T > R > P > S$; the transformation is a strict
sign flip and preserves the strategic ordering. The whole matrix is then
multiplied by $\lam$, exactly as in the experimental protocol, so that the two
sides of the comparison are anchored to the same per-round magnitudes.

For a resident strategy $i$ and a mutant $j$, the expected per-round payoff
$\pi_{ij}$ is obtained analytically over the same horizon the experiments use,
$r = 10$ rounds, by propagating the joint distribution over the two players'
actions forward round by round and averaging; no simulation enters. The
fixation probability of a single $j$-mutant in a resident $i$-population is
\begin{equation}
\rho_{ji} \;=\; \left(1 + \sum_{k=1}^{Z-1}\prod_{m=1}^{k}
   \frac{T^{-}_{ji}(m)}{T^{+}_{ji}(m)}\right)^{-1},
\label{eq:fixation}
\end{equation}
where $T^{\pm}_{ji}(m)$ are the Fermi-weighted transition rates at composition
$m$~\cite{szabo1998evolutionary}. Assembling these probabilities gives an
embedded Markov chain over the four monomorphic states whose unique stationary
distribution $\sigma$ is the long-run frequency of each strategy in the
rare-mutation regime~\cite{sigmund:2010bo}. The reference implementation is
the open-source EGTtools library~\cite{domingos2023egttools}.

\subsection{Deterministic continuous dynamics and simplex orbits}\label{appendix:egt_simplex}

In the infinite-population deterministic limit, evolutionary dynamics is
described by the standard replicator equation~\cite{sigmund:2010bo,nowak2006five}:
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}_i \;=\; x_i \left[ (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})_i - \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x} \right],
\label{eq:replicator}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_4)^T$ is the population state on the
three-simplex $\Delta_3$ spanned by the four canonical rules (AllC, AllD, TFT,
WSLS), and $\mathbf{A}$ is the $4 \times 4$ payoff matrix. Under a scalar payoff
transformation $\mathbf{A}' = \lam \mathbf{A}$ with $\lam > 0$, the vector field
scales uniformly:
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}_i' \;=\; \lam \, \dot{x}_i.
\label{eq:replicator_scaled}
\end{equation}
Multiplying the payoff matrix by a positive constant multiplies the replicator
vector field by that constant. This changes the velocity of state evolution along
trajectories by a uniform time reparametrisation $d\tau = \lam \, dt$, but leaves
every trajectory, orbit geometry, invariant manifold, and rest point strictly
invariant.

Figure~\ref{fig:app_simplex} illustrates this mathematical invariance across the
four faces of the three-simplex spanned by the canonical rules, leaving one rule
out per face. Across 2{,}000 randomly sampled population states, the normalised
vector fields at $\lam = 0.01$ and $\lam = 1000$ agree to within
$4 \times 10^{-14}$, confirming that continuous deterministic selection cannot
account for any empirical dependence on payoff scale.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/f_simplex.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The deterministic dynamics do not see the payoff scale.}
Replicator flow on each of the four faces of the three-simplex spanned by the
canonical rules, one face per strategy left out, at $\lam = 1$,

\subsection{The strategies under execution noise}\label{appendix:egt_strategies}

The four canonical rules enter as memory-one policies with a per-round
implementation error $\varepsilon$, the probability of executing the opposite
of the intended action. Writing $\mathrm{hi} = 1 - \varepsilon$ and
$\mathrm{lo} = \varepsilon$, the probability of cooperating is $\mathrm{hi}$
wherever table~\ref{tab:A-rules} prescribes $C$ and $\mathrm{lo}$ wherever it
prescribes $D$, including on the opening move, so AllC, Tit-for-Tat and
Win-Stay-Lose-Shift open at $\mathrm{hi}$ and AllD at $\mathrm{lo}$. The rules
are therefore the same objects the empirical read-out matches against, which is
what makes the two sides of the comparison commensurable.

\subsection{Numerical method and precision audit}\label{appendix:egt_numerics}

The computation is numerically delicate at the top of the payoff grid, and we
report the audit rather than only the result. Equation~\eqref{eq:fixation}
accumulates a product of $Z-1$ Fermi ratios whose exponents scale with
$\beta \lam \Delta\pi$; at the largest scales that product overflows the range
of double precision, every fixation probability rounds to exactly 0 or 1, the
embedded chain acquires more than one absorbing state, and the eigenvector for
eigenvalue 1 is no longer unique. A double-precision solver then returns an
arbitrary member of a degenerate eigenspace, which is a wrong answer that
carries no warning.

The published grid is therefore computed twice: once in arbitrary precision
with \texttt{mpmath} at 200 decimal digits, which is the primary solver, and
once in double precision through EGTtools, which serves as a check. Each cell
of the grid records the maximum absolute discrepancy between the two solvers
and the number of absorbing states the double-precision chain possesses, so a
reader can see exactly where double precision fails and by how much.

\subsection{Finite-population invasion structure across scales}\label{appendix:egt_invasion}

In contrast to the continuous deterministic limit, the payoff scale $\lam$
acts directly on finite populations governed by stochastic imitation. Under the
Fermi update rule, the probability that an individual adopting strategy $i$
switches to strategy $j$ upon pairwise comparison is:
\begin{equation}
P(i \to j) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(-\beta (\pi_j - \pi_i)\right)}
\;=\; \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(-\beta \lam (\pi_j^{(0)} - \pi_i^{(0)})\right)},
\label{eq:fermi_scaling}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_j^{(0)}$ denotes baseline unscaled payoffs. The payoff scale $\lam$
and the selection intensity $\beta$ enter the update rule exclusively through
their product $\beta \lam$. Consequently, scaling the payoffs by $\lam$ is
formally equivalent to scaling the selection pressure.

Figure~\ref{fig:app_invasion} shows the resulting invasion graphs across the
four monomorphic states as $\lam$ varies. At $\lam = 0.01$, selection is
negligible, and transition probabilities approach neutral drift ($1/Z = 0.01$).
As $\lam$ increases to baseline ($\lam = 1.0$) and high stakes ($\lam = 10.0$),
selection sharpens, making AllD an evolutionary sink that resists invasion from
all three cooperative rules and fixes with high probability.

\subsection{Comparison with the corpus}\label{appendix:egt_vs_llm}

Figure~\ref{fig:egt_vs_llm} places the stationary distribution beside the
pooled label composition of the corpus at each payoff scale.

Two features agree. Both put their greatest behavioural diversity at the
smallest payoff scales, where the baseline is close to neutral drift because
$\beta \lam \Delta\pi$ is small and the corpus shows its models at their most
dissimilar. And in both, a change in $\lam$ moves mass between the conditional
cooperators and unconditional defection rather than reshuffling the four
strategies arbitrarily.

The direction is the disagreement, and it is the whole point of the comparison.
The baseline drives Always Defect upward as the payoff scale grows, because a
larger $\lam$ multiplies the payoff differences that the Fermi rule responds to
and so sharpens selection against any rule that can be exploited; at
$\varepsilon = 0.05$ its share runs from 0.267 at $\lam = 0.01$ to 1.000 by
$\lam = 100$, and the process ends with the population entirely on Always
Defect (table~\ref{tab:C-egt-vs-llm}). The corpus does the opposite in
aggregate: its pooled AllD share falls from $43.6\%$ at $\lam = 0.01$ to
$28.3\%$ at $\lam = 10^{3}$, and its AllC share rises from $38.1\%$ to
$45.8\%$. The two objects, given the same matrices and restricted to the same
four rules, predict qualitatively different worlds depending on who is playing.

One qualification belongs with the baseline rather than with the comparison.
The rise of Always Defect in $\lam$ is monotone at every positive execution
error, but not in the error-free corner $\varepsilon = 0$, where the share falls
from 0.319 at $\lam = 0.1$ to 0.273 at $\lam = 2$, a dip of 0.046, before rising
(table~\ref{tab:C-egt-alld}). The reason is
specific and worth stating, because it is a property of the model and not of
the arithmetic. With no execution error, AllC, Tit-for-Tat and
Win-Stay-Lose-Shift earn exactly $-2\lam$ against each other and against
themselves, so the three cooperative rules are neutral among themselves and the
ordering is decided entirely by their exchanges with Always Defect. Over ten
rounds Tit-for-Tat loses only its opening move to Always Defect, whereas
Win-Stay-Lose-Shift keeps being re-exploited and AllC is exploited throughout,
so raising $\lam$ first sharpens selection in Tit-for-Tat's favour before
Always Defect's one-round advantage finally decides it. Any positive error rate
removes the dip, because it lets Always Defect exploit the retaliation lag
repeatedly rather than once. The empirically implied error rates are all
positive and well above zero (\S\ref{appendix:egt_calibration}), so the
qualification does not touch the comparison.

A second disagreement is structural rather than directional. The baseline is
language-blind by construction: there is nothing in equation~\eqref{eq:fixation}
for a prompt language to enter. In the corpus, the interaction of the model
with the prompt language carries $17.0\%$ of the variance across cells, more
than the payoff scale carries as a main effect. Whatever the corpus is
responding to, a payoff matrix is not a complete description of it.

We read the gap as a measurement and not as a falsification. It sizes what
these agents bring that an evolutionary model does not, from alignment training
to the cooperative bias of a human text corpus and the connotations the prompt
language carries. Treating the baseline as the null and the gap as the quantity
to be explained is, we would suggest, a productive way to read studies of
language models in strategic settings.

In a study with no empirical read-out, $\varepsilon$ would have to be chosen.

analogue of an implementation error (table~\ref{tab:A-egtcalib}).

\subsection{Robustness of the evolutionary baseline across 80 settings}\label{appendix:robust_egt}

The finding that the evolutionary baseline predicts an increase in defection
with payoff scale is not an artefact of the specific baseline parameters
($Z = 100$, $\beta = 0.1$, $\varepsilon = 0.05$). To confirm this, we solved the
stationary distribution across a comprehensive grid of 80 parameter combinations:
four population sizes $Z \in \{50, 100, 200, 500\}$, five selection intensities
$\beta \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0\}$, and four execution noise levels
$\varepsilon \in \{0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2\}$, across all ten payoff scales (800 cells
in total, each computed at 200 decimal digits).

Figure~\ref{fig:app_robustness} presents this parameter sweep. Across all 80
settings, the stationary share of Always Defect increases with $\lam$, exceeding
$90\%$ at large scales in every setting, with a median increase of 0.716 and a
minimum increase of 0.195. In 61 of the 80 settings the rise is strictly
monotone; the 19 non-monotone cases occur exclusively at $\varepsilon = 0$,
where a transient dip occurs before selection drives AllD to fixation
(\S\ref{appendix:egt_vs_llm}). The pooled empirical corpus moves in the
opposite direction to this theoretical prediction. This pooled reversal is
driven primarily by GPT and Grok; per-model label trajectories are heterogeneous.

\subsection{The read-out at the edge of its validated range}\label{appendix:robust_readout}

The classifier was validated to $20\%$ execution noise, which is two rounds in
ten. Table~\ref{tab:A-egtcalib} shows that Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.4 Nano
sit at 2.07 and 2.12 rounds from the nearest canonical rule on average, so for
those two models the read-out is operating at the very edge of the range it was
validated on, and their label compositions are the weakest evidence in this
article.

The two claims that matter most are insulated from this. The falling share of
agent-games matched exactly by one rule, and the rising number of rounds
violating the nearest rule, are both computed by exact matching with no learned
component at all, and it is precisely those two quantities that the main text
uses to argue that play becomes less rule-governed. The compositional claim,
which does use the classifier, is reported with its provenance beside it
(table~\ref{tab:S-provenance}) and is described in the main text as the weaker
of the two.

There is a further and more general caveat. A strategy is a decision rule and
not a decision, so it is never observed; separating the memory-one rules from
observed play has been shown to require a long run of observations even where
the resident rule is fixed by construction~\cite{kim2021win,hilbe2013memory}.
Our trajectories are ten rounds long. This is why the read-out is reported
throughout as a resemblance and never as an identification, and why richer
strategy vocabularies, including zero-determinant and extortionate
play~\cite{pressDyson2012}, and mixture estimators designed for longer
horizons~\cite{fontana2025nicer,inferenceStrategies}, are named as the natural
extension rather than attempted here.

\subsection{Parsing, fallback and the direction of the bias}\label{appendix:robust_parsing}

The parsing fallback records an unresolvable reply as Option~A, which is
defection under this framing (\S\ref{appendix:endpoints}). Any contamination it
introduces therefore lowers a measured cooperation rate. Two consequences
follow. The cooperative behaviour reported in the main text cannot be an
artefact of the fallback, because the fallback cannot produce cooperation. And
if the fallback rate were correlated with the payoff scale, it would attenuate
a measured range rather than create one, so the smallest ranges in
table~\ref{tab:effect} are the ones such a correlation could most plausibly
distort, and they are also the ones the article leans on least.

\subsection{What this design does not measure}\label{appendix:robust_gaps}

Two limits are worth naming so that they are not mistaken for findings.
First, an independent repeat of the Gemini 3.5 arm at $\lam \in
\{0.1,0.25,0.5,1\}$ provides a limited test-retest check. The absolute
between-run differences in cooperation were 0.002, 0.014, 0.002 and 0.038,
respectively. This result supports repeatability for those four conditions but
does not provide a reproducibility floor for other models, languages or scales.

Second, clustering on the dyad handles the dependence between the two agents of
a game and between the rounds inside an agent-game, but it is not a
mixed-effects model with random intercepts for replicate and game, which would
be the fuller treatment of a nested design. Because the direction of every
effect is consistent across conditions and the clustered intervals are wider
than their unclustered counterparts, we regard the reported inference as
conservative rather than optimistic, but we do not claim it is complete.
The matched-block randomisation provides a complementary sensitivity analysis,
not a replacement for a collection record that preserves provider seed metadata.

The release also contains a targeted notation control for Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite.
At fixed payoff magnitude, changing two decimal places to three changed the
cooperation rate by $0.0073$ at $\lambda=0.1$ ($p=0.84$) and by $0.011$ at
$\lambda=1$ ($p=0.68$). The corresponding magnitude contrast from $0.1$ to $1$
under three-decimal formatting was $0.096$ ($p=0.0018$). This small control
does not prove that notation is irrelevant, but it weakens the claim that a
single trailing-zero convention explains the main scale effect.

\subsection{Matched scale ladders and influence checks}\label{appendix:robust_ladders}

The scale labels can also be permuted within each complete game-ID ladder. This
is a stricter null than the main analysis because it preserves the recorded
matching across all ten scales. With 10,000 blocked permutations, the observed
scale range remains significant for every model: $p=0.0023$ for Claude and
$p=0.0001$ for each of GPT, Gemini 3.1, Gemini 3.5, Grok and Qwen. The ranges
are unchanged because the test changes only the null assignment. The complete
results are included in the released analysis tables.

As a complementary influence check, removing each model in turn leaves a
significant pooled scale effect. The scale-test $p$-values are below $0.01$ in
all six leave-one-model-out fits, with pooled ranges from $0.048$ to $0.159$.
Thus pooled cancellation is not caused by a single model, even though the
magnitude of the pooled range changes. The complete results are included in the
released analysis tables.

\subsection{Uncertainty in the conditioning contrasts}\label{appendix:conditioning_ci}

The main text compares the relative size of partner conditioning and own-action
conditioning. To show the uncertainty directly, table~\ref{tab:conditioning-ci}
reports percentile intervals from 4,000 resamples of whole dyads. The resampling
unit keeps both agents and all ten rounds of a game together. Reciprocity is the
partner main effect, persistence is the own-action main effect, and matching is
their interaction. The intervals confirm that persistence is positive in every
model, whereas reciprocity is positive in four models and negative in Gemini
3.1 and Qwen. These are descriptive contrasts and do not identify a causal
mechanism.

The persistence comparison is stable to the context-count rule. Across minimum
context thresholds of 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 observations, the mean
persistence-minus-reciprocity contrast remains positive for every model. A
Jeffreys-smoothed estimate using all observed contexts gives the same result.
The complete threshold grid is included in the released analysis tables. The result is strongest for
Gemini 3.1, Gemini 3.5, Grok and Qwen; Claude's average difference is small and
some individual cells are close, so we interpret the title's comparison as a
relative corpus-level tendency rather than a universal cell-level rule.

Finally, a round-level logistic model with scale, language, both persona terms
and their scale-by-language interactions rejects the joint null of no
scale-by-language interaction for every model. The largest Holm-adjusted
$p$-value is $1.4\times10^{-48}$, so the interaction is not only a visual
range difference. Its interpretation remains conditional on the language
templates used here, including the documented Arabic and Chinese wording
difference. Full coefficients and adjusted tests are included in the released
analysis tables.

\section{Reproduction}\label{appendix:repro}

Every number, figure and table in this article and in this appendix is produced
by an archived pipeline from the released transcript corpus. Tables and figures
are generated directly; numerical values quoted manually in prose are checked
against the data by the verification ledger. The three main-text tables are
generated into \texttt{tables\_auto.tex}, the supplementary tables of
\S\ref{appendix:results} into \texttt{supp\_tables\_auto.tex}, and the
evolutionary grid of \S\ref{appendix:egt} into \texttt{egt\_tables\_auto.tex}.

The pipeline runs in the following order. \texttt{s00\_build} reads the
transcript corpus, applies the completeness guard of
\S\ref{appendix:corpus} and writes the tidy game, round and read-out tables.
\texttt{s01\_train\_lstm} trains the classifier of \S\ref{appendix:classifier}
on the synthetic corpus. \texttt{s02\_readout} applies the rule base and the
classifier to the transcripts. \texttt{s03\_stats} and
\texttt{s04\_strategy\_stats} compute every statistic reported here.
\texttt{s05\_figures} and \texttt{s05b\_appendix\_figures} draw the figures
directly into the manuscript directory. \texttt{s06\_tables} and
\texttt{s07\_supplementary} render the tables. \texttt{s09\_egt} computes the
evolutionary grid of \S\ref{appendix:egt}. Finally \texttt{s08\_verify\_paper}
recomputes the ledger of headline quantities quoted in the manuscript prose
from the data and fails if any of them disagrees, so that a stale number cannot
survive an edit.

Three seeds fix the stochastic parts: 12345 for the corpus build, 1234 for
classifier training, and 20260905 for the bootstrap and permutation draws.

The results reported here were produced under Python 3.12.3 with NumPy 1.26.4,
pandas 2.2.2, PyArrow 16.1.0, SciPy 1.17.1, statsmodels 0.14.6,
scikit-learn 1.5.1, PyTorch 2.11.0 on CPU, Matplotlib 3.9.2, mpmath 1.3.0 and
EGTtools 0.1.14.2. The classifier is trained on CPU and its seed is fixed, so
the read-out is reproducible without a GPU.
